# Te Manawa — Ecology of the Manawatū: Research Outline

**Status: outline only.** This is the skeleton we fill in habitat by habitat. Each
numbered sub-habitat below gets its own deep dive later; what's here is the sketch,
the evidence quality, and the specific things that need checking against post-1980
work.

**Framing (agreed):** build the picture from the **pre-European (~1840) landscape**,
which is the only period with real species-level detail, then **extrapolate to deep
time where that's defensible**. Treat ~1840 as a *late-interglacial snapshot* — a
reasonable analogue for MIS 5e / MIS 11 lowland Manawatū — and note for each habitat
how it would shift in a glacial. The deep-time dives come after; this document is
their groundwork.

**Slant:** ecology first. Build implications are flagged inline as `[BUILD]`.

**Asset scope (constraint on every deep dive):** each deep dive ends with a shortlist
of **2–4 representative plant species per habitat**, chosen for silhouette contrast at
screen scale as well as ecological correctness. More only where a species is shared
across habitats. Everything below that threshold becomes ground texture, not entities.

**Fauna:** scored longlist and recommended cast in **`TEMANAWA_FAUNA.md`**.

## 0. The source, and what it can and can't tell us

**A. E. Esler, *Botany of the Manawatū District, New Zealand*, DSIR Information
Series 127 (Government Printer, Wellington, 1978).** The five PDFs are its
history/context, forests, shrublands, grasslands, dunes and flora sections.

**How Esler reconstructed the past.** There is *no* botanical account of any part of
the Manawatū before 1920. His reconstruction rests on four legs, and he is unusually
honest about all four being weak:

1. **Survey plans drawn 1859–1881** (Lands & Survey, listed by surveyor + S.O.
   number). Intensive farming hadn't started, so boundaries are roughly pre-modification.
   J. T. Stewart's 2 inch-to-the-mile river surveys are the *least* accurate; the
   10 chain-to-the-inch blocks west of the Ōroua are good. Forest margins are
   reliable; **swamp margins are not**, and **shrubland is barely mapped at all**.
2. **Passing comments by travellers** — Kettle (1842), E. J. Wakefield (1845),
   Richard Taylor (1849), Buick (1903). A handful of sentences each.
3. **Interviews with old residents** in the 1960s (Milligan, Dear, Barber, Thompson,
   Keeble, Hayes) — a genuinely irreplaceable source, and the reason the book exists.
4. **Present-day relics read backwards**, with all the distortion that implies.

**The four biases to carry through everything below:**

- **Timber selection.** Tōtara, kahikatea, rimu and mātai were logged first. Remnants
  are systematically *depleted* in the species that defined the forest. Tawa is
  over-represented today for exactly this reason — Esler notes Kitchener Park almost
  certainly has more tawa now than before milling.
- **Durability bias.** The record of vanished forest is stumps and logs, so it
  favours rot-resistant tōtara, northern rātā, black maire, and under-counts kāmahi
  (which Esler thinks was a major canopy tree that left almost no trace).
- **Introduced browsers.** Possums (main liberation near Tiritea ~1907, numerous by
  1921) destroyed the northern rātā–kāmahi forest during Esler's own fieldwork.
  Cattle, deer, pigs and sheep restructured every understorey he describes.
- **Pre-1840 human fire.** Esler repeatedly suspects Māori burning — the Mt Stewart
  downlands, some non-swampy plains clearings, a fire through the Tiritea catchment
  ~1760 — but couldn't date it. Modern palynology can (§4).

`[BUILD]` The honest position for the installation: **the composition of the wet
forest and the dunes is well constrained; the composition of the dry plains
grassland and the sand-country shrubland is essentially unknown**, and Esler says so
outright. Anything we render there is inference.

## 1. The stage — physical setting

Filled in briefly as context for all three habitat groups.

- **Coast:** ~19 km of sand within Esler's survey area, part of a much longer
  dunefield. Fed by littoral drift carrying erosion debris from the Rangitīkei,
  Whangaehu and Whanganui rivers. The coast was *prograding* at 0.6–1.0 m/yr.
- **River flats:** the Rangitīkei, Manawatū and Ōroua built about **half** the
  Manawatū lowlands. The Manawatū drains 594,600 ha from both flanks of the Ruahine
  and Tararua, then winds ~80 km below 60 m a.s.l. **Shingle bed to about Palmerston
  North; fine silty alluvium and true meanders below that.** That break is the single
  most important ecological line on the plains.
- **Peat swamps** filled large low-lying tracts, "overlying relics of forest in many
  places" — buried drowned forest is a recurring motif (§2.6).
- **Terraces** in three blocks (east of the river against the Tararua; NE of
  Palmerston North; between Ōroua and Rangitīkei). The higher two are **deeply
  mantled in loess blown off the Manawatū and Rangitīkei beds during the last
  glaciation** — a direct, usable link between the game's cold phases and soil
  fertility.
- **Climate (Palmerston North):** Jan mean 16.6 °C, June 8.1 °C, ~60 ground frosts,
  173 rain days, 1,814 sunshine hours. Rainfall ~750 mm at the coast rising steeply
  into the Tararua; coast >2,000 sunshine hours, Tararua ~1,400. **Very strong,
  persistent W/NW winds — ~320 wind days a year at PN, gales possible in any month.**
- **Flora totals (1978):** 1,033 wild taxa; 505 indigenous, of which 354 NZ endemics;
  85 ferns, all but one native. Largest families Poaceae 152, Asteraceae 145,
  Cyperaceae 74.

`[BUILD]` Wind is the most under-used environmental driver available to us and it is
*characteristic* of this district, not generic. The 1936 gale halved the emergent
trees in Keebles Bush. Salt-laden westerlies drive the whole dune system and shape
the montane forest edge.

## 2. Group A — Lowland and the forests around the Manawatū River

Forest covered roughly **half** the district. Esler splits it into seven types; the
first three plus the wetlands belong here.

### 2.1 Kahikatea–pukatea semi-swamp forest
*Modern name: kahikatea, pukatea forest (Singers & Rogers WF8).*

The signature lowland forest. Most extensive in the **Taonui basin**, inside the
levées of the Manawatū and Ōroua above their confluence; elsewhere a mosaic with
other types along river margins. Kahikatea on the finest, permanently wet sediments;
tōtara on the higher, coarser levées, often within sight of each other.

Structure from Round Bush and Himatangi Bush: pukatea to ~28 m at ~4.7 stems/100 m²,
kiekie festooning to 18 m, *Astelia hastata* clumps "like huge birds' nests" in the
crowns, *Griselinia lucida*, and four *Metrosideros* vines. Understorey richness
tracks light and wetness; the wettest floor is nearly bare but for *Ranunculus
rivularis* and *Callitriche*. **Supplejack thickets acted as browse refugia** —
protecting whole assemblages on mounds above winter water level.

Deep dive covers: the mound/hollow microtopography, the kahikatea "walk on the roots
dryshod" flooded-forest imagery from Cockayne and Aston, and the sand-country variant.

### 2.2 Tōtara forest of levées, terraces and the dry downlands

> ✅ **Deep dive complete — see `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY_LOWLAND.md`**, covering §2.2–2.4
> and §2.6, plus episodic podocarp recruitment, the moa-browse question, and a full
> **then-and-now** section.

Esler: "not a vestige remains as it was a century ago." Reconstructed almost entirely
from stumps, place names and interviews. Ran the **riparian levées** of the Manawatū
and Ōroua, and formed what was probably the largest single block on the
**Rongotea–Ohakea terraces** — a district with **<914 mm rain, occasional drought and
constant drying wind, sitting at the climatic margin between forest and grassland.**

That marginality is the interesting part: Esler thinks tōtara forest here was
destroyed and re-destroyed — first by shifting Manawatū river levels, later by Māori
fire — and regenerated slowly, so the "scrub country" of mānuka, toetoe, koromiko,
tutu and bracken the settlers found was **recently burnt forest, not natural
shrubland**, evidenced by stumps under the scrub.

Associates recalled: mātai, miro, rimu, and **very large northern rātā near
Rongotea** (still burning 25 years after clearance).

`[BUILD]` This is our best-evidenced **fire-edge / ecotone** habitat and the natural
home of a forest↔shrubland flip in the sim.

### 2.3 Mixed podocarp–broadleaf forest

Intermediate between §2.1 and §2.2 — rimu, mātai, tōtara, kahikatea, miro over an
invariably tawa-containing canopy. Occupied recent alluvium, the terraces east of the
river, and the Tiritea/Kahuterawa headwater basins.

**Keebles Bush** is the reference stand: emergent podocarps >30 m (rimu most
conspicuous, mātai as numerous, then kahikatea/tōtara/miro), **main canopy only
9–15 m** of tawa and māhoe with tītoki, kāmahi, rewarewa, hīnau, mapou, lancewood.
Kawakawa-dominant shrub layer; 29 fern species; podocarp regeneration negligible.

The 1868 surveyor's line on the Linton terraces — "Fine level land. Rimu and tawa
predominate" — is one of the few direct descriptions of this forest standing.

### 2.4 Tawa forest

Tawa is in every forest type except beech, and takes over where podocarps are
removed. **Critically, Esler argues the swing to tawa dominance was already underway
over many centuries before milling** — podocarps were failing to recruit — and
Europeans merely accelerated it. That's a slow-succession fact worth building in.

Best stands: the Manawatū Gorge State Forest (pure tawa canopy >30 m, almost no
understorey under the closed canopy) and the Tiritea reserve — **the best-preserved
forest of any kind in the district**, sheltered from the prevailing wind.

### 2.5 Swamps, peat and the wet plains

> ✅ **Deep dive complete — see `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY_WETLAND.md`**, which also covers
> §2.1 (swamp forest), §2.6 (the river) and §4.5 (lagoons), and contains the redesigns
> for the Growth and Eruption buttons.

**>8,000 ha, over 8% of the land surface — and Esler flags that as an underestimate**
because swamp margins were poorly mapped. Named systems: **Makerua** (between the
Manawatū and the Linton–Tokomaru terraces), **Moutoa**, the **Taonui basin** group
(Taonui, Kōwhai, Te Waiti), and the swamps of the coastal lagoons (§4.5).

The zonation (Poole & Boyce 1949, Moutoa) is the most useful single data structure in
the whole book — **an entire vegetation sequence across ~1.8 m of elevation**:

```
open water → Carex secta → raupō → harakeke (Phormium) → swamp scrub
             (Cordyline australis + Coprosma spp.) → kahikatea/pukatea semi-swamp forest
```

Stewart's survey note on Makerua — *"peat 10 feet under surface, a pole being pushed
in"* — and the post-drainage subsidence of a metre or more, exposing **stumps of an
extensive drowned forest**, is the key image: this landscape *drowned its own forest*
repeatedly.

`[BUILD]` Harakeke expanded *after* drainage, so the flax-dominated Manawatū of the
photographs is partly an artefact. Pre-European, toetoe likely held the wetter
ground.

### 2.6 The river itself — shingle, backwaters, drowned forest

- **Shingle beds:** >70 species colonise the domes at low flow, most washed away by
  the next flood. Native constant: *Raoulia tenuicaulis* var. *dimorpha*, the only
  plant specific to this habitat.
- **Slow reaches and cut-offs:** a distinct aquatic flora — *Myriophyllum*,
  *Potamogeton*, *Ruppia*.
- **Drowned tōtara forest:** stumps *in situ* with roots at "Go-to-Hell Swamp"
  between East Base trig and Himatangi Bush, 1.8–3.6 m apart under 1.8 m of peat.
  Esler rejects the "gale snapped them" reading and argues a **rising water table
  killed the forest** — plausibly because the river's outfall was periodically
  restricted by sand at the mouth or by **growth of the Whirokino anticline** (Rich
  1959, Te Punga 1957).

`[BUILD]` A tectonically-dammed river drowning its own floodplain forest is a
ready-made deep-time event, and it is *local and evidenced* rather than invented.

## 3. Group B — Bush, shrubland and grassland

### 3.1 Northern rātā–kāmahi forest, upper Tararua flanks

The montane forest, and the one Esler watched die. Reconstructed from stumps plus
F. G. Hayes' 1927 recollections. **Multi-tiered:** northern rātā, miro and rimu
emergent over a subordinate layer of kāmahi, tawa and black maire; **above ~365 m,
*Griselinia littoralis* and Hall's tōtara come in and tawa drops out.** Rātā >30 m
stood on ridges that now carry cropped turf.

Hayes on the 1927 understorey: *"Forest was very open... floor of the forest was very
clean. No seedlings or young trees except in odd small patches... No pig or goat
sign, but deer were numerous."*

Collapse sequence (Esler 1969c, his figure 23): possums defoliate kāmahi → death
within a year or two → canopy opens → ground browsers remove all regeneration →
shrubland → tussock/turf. **Complete in roughly three decades.**

### 3.2 The subalpine cap — *Olearia colensoi* on Arawaru

Above ~670 m kāmahi/Hall's tōtara gave way to leatherwood scrub to the 767 m summit.
Notable because **at least 11 plant species occur here and nowhere else in the
district** — this is the one genuinely subalpine element in the flora, and the
obvious refuge for cold-phase species during interglacials.

### 3.3 Black beech at Aokautere — the anomaly

~526 ha, 90–275 m, on **strongly leached terrace soils**, crossing from terrace onto
greywacke; the only beech in the survey area. A little hard beech, and hybrids.
Worth a dive on its own: beech distribution in the southern North Island is patchy in
a way that reflects **glacial refugia and slow post-glacial spread**, which makes this
patch directly relevant to deep time.

### 3.4 Shrublands — natural, or burnt forest?

> ✅ **Deep dive complete — see `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY_OPEN.md`**, covering §3.4–3.5 plus
> the glacial shrub–grassland mosaic. **It contains a design correction: most of the
> grassland and seral shrubland below is fire-induced and post-1280 CE, so it does not
> belong in the installation's window.**

Esler's own framing: shrubland formed "where fire or other factors prevented woody
vegetation running the full course of succession to forest," and he can only call
three cases arguably natural — the wet sand plains, the river margins, and the
Arawaru summit. Everything else he thinks is secondary.

Types: mānuka (dominant on leached terraces), **kānuka on the dunes**, bracken, and a
distinctive **"miki" shrubland of *Coprosma propinqua*, *C. robusta* and their hybrid
plus *Olearia virgata*** on the black sands of the second dune phase, extending
inland from the coastal lagoons — cleared only 30–50 years before Esler wrote.

Two succession models are described in enough detail to implement directly:
**regeneration through bracken** (pasture → bracken → pioneer shrubs → tree ferns →
tawa/kāmahi/māhoe) and **regeneration through mānuka** (Watt's pioneer / building /
mature / degenerate stages, with light at ground level as the controlling variable).

`[BUILD]` These two are the cleanest succession rulesets in the book and worth
lifting almost verbatim into the plant/growth model.

### 3.5 Grassland — the biggest hole in the record

**Esler: "There were grasslands in the Manawatū in Māori times but we know nothing of
their composition."** The evidence is three sentences:

- Wakefield, 1840: *"We travelled all day through open pasture land, the path
  apparently avoiding the timber parts."*
- Taylor, 1849: a level grass plain extending as far as the eye could see.
- Buick, 1903, on Foxton–Ōroua Bridge: *"a succession of sand-hills, which rose in
  long undulating ridges, like great billows of the sea... grass-covered dunes...
  there grew in great abundance the manuka, toetoe, and tussock, while between the
  sandy ridges there would sometimes nestle acres of rich soil, or perhaps a swampy
  marsh fringed with green rushes and raupo."*

Esler poses but cannot answer **why this land carried grassland rather than scrub**,
and his species list is explicitly *"pure conjecture"*: *Microlaena stipoides*,
*Rytidosperma* spp., *Poa anceps*, with *Geranium*, *Acaena*, *Carex*, *Pimelea
prostrata*, *Ficinia nodosa*, *Muehlenbeckia complexa*.

`[BUILD]` Buick's line is the best single description of the pre-European sand
country we have, and it's a **mosaic**, not a grassland: grassy dune ridges,
mānuka/toetoe/tussock, and swampy hollows between. Build the mosaic, not a biome.

## 4. Group C — The coast and dune lands

> ✅ **Deep dive complete — see `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY_COAST.md`** for the full treatment,
> revised Koputaroa chronology, the wind/storm spec and the coastal flora shortlist.
> The summary below stands; the deep dive supersedes it on detail.

The strongest section of the book, and the one where Esler is working from direct
observation rather than reconstruction.

### 4.1 Dune-building phases — **update needed**

Esler works with **three** phases (his 1st = active coastal strip; 2nd = plains and
long low parabolic dunes stable 5+ centuries; 3rd = reaching 19 km inland, movement
ceased ~3,000–5,000 yr BP). The standard framework since Cowie (1963) has **four**,
and the fourth is the one that matters most for us:

| Phase | Approx. age | Esler's equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **Koputaroa** | late Pleistocene – early Holocene, ~20–10 ka | *absent* |
| **Foxton** | pre-Taupō Tephra, ~4,000–2,000 yr BP | 3rd phase |
| **Motuiti** | ~1,000–500 yr BP | 2nd phase |
| **Waitārere** | from ~100 yr BP, still accumulating | 1st phase |

**Koputaroa is the deep-time hook** — a glacial-age dune-building episode, exactly
the mechanism the build plan already assumes (falling sea level → exposed shelf →
inland sand advance → dammed drainage → dune lakes). Deep dive should chase the
optical/TL dating literature (Shepherd; Hesp; Duller) rather than the 1963 estimates,
since these ages have been revised.

### 4.2 The active fore dune — three plants, three landforms

The most directly game-usable finding in the book: **each sand-binder builds a
measurably different dune.**

| Species | Dune form | Height | Seaward slope |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spinifex** (kōwhangatara) | smooth, even, regular | ~6 m | 14–16° |
| **Marram** (*introduced ~1890s*) | stepped, steep, blow-out prone | to ~8 m | 24–28° |
| **Pīngao** (*Ficinia spiralis*) | smooth, convex face, level top | seldom >3 m | 8–14° |

Mechanism is explained properly: spinifex's diffuse flexible cover calms wind evenly
and its rhizomes run *down* slopes to the dune base preventing wave erosion; marram's
clumping habit funnels wind between clumps and undercuts itself.

**Pre-European, marram doesn't exist.** The natural fore dune is a spinifex/pīngao
system — lower, smoother, more mobile.

### 4.3 Sand plains, deflation and the parabolic dune machine

The geometry: sand deflates down to the **water table**, which slopes evenly at ~2 m
per km and sets the floor for the whole system. Parabolic dunes advance, trailing
arms break away and become longitudinal dunes, adjacent plains coalesce into basins
bounded by a rear dune. `[BUILD]` This is a proper procedural landform rule, not
decoration.

### 4.4 Dune slacks and turf communities

Wet hollows behind the rear dune colonise in a fixed sequence: *Myriophyllum
votschii*, *Limosella lineata*, *Eleocharis neozelandica*, *Ranunculus acaulis* and
*Selliera* on the wettest ground; **a *Carex pumila* fringe that builds its own low
mound ~0.3 m high**; then oioi (*Apodasmia similis*) dominant with *Schoenus nitens*;
then toetoe, cabbage tree, sometimes mānuka and *Olearia solandri* at ~50 years.

**Modern check:** these turf wetlands are now known to be genuinely mobile — recent
work at Tangimoana, Foxton Beach and Himatangi measures them **migrating inland
~2.7 m/yr while wobbling ~5.2 m/yr**. Esler intuited the dynamism; it's now
quantified.

### 4.5 The coastal lagoon chain

Dunes moving inland dammed drainage off the gently sloping hinterland, producing a
string of lagoons ~3 km behind the beach — **Pukepuke, Koputara, Kaikokopu**, with
extensive swamp around each, and older buried lagoons beneath (a compressed peat band
3.5–4.5 m down near Round Bush). Pukepuke is <0.6 m deep with raupō rafting out from
the margins over an open-water sequence of *Myriophyllum*, *Potamogeton*, *Ruppia*,
*Azolla*, *Lemna* and *Wolffia*.

`[BUILD]` Dune-dammed lakes appearing and vanishing as the dunefield advances is
already in the terrain plan; this gives it real named systems and a real plant list.

### 4.6 Forest on the dunes

Only ~2,100 ha of forest on the sand country, mostly >8 km inland; Cockayne's flat
"forest is rare on the dunes" is too strong. **No single species characterises it —
the *combination* does:** tawa, māhoe, tītoki, **akeake (*Dodonaea viscosa*) and
ngaio** as physiognomic dominants, with rewarewa, mapou, kaikōmako, hīnau, lancewood,
pigeonwood, kōwhai, *Pittosporum tenuifolium* and kānuka.

Esler believes tōtara grew on the older dunes but says the evidence is "very slender"
(massive tōtara on third-phase dunes at Whites Bush, nowhere else) — and suspects
fire plus timber-hunger removed it.

### 4.7 Estuary and saltmarsh

From H. H. Allan's 1924 description: *Juncus maritimus* var. *australiensis* and oioi
as co-dominants in extensive sheets, with *Sarcocornia*, *Triglochin striata* and
*Scirpus americanus* on the mud fringe. Also a **salt meadow of *Sarcocornia* growing
on raised circular sand mounds**, which Esler says resembles Farewell Spit more than
anything else he knows — reached only by the highest tides, and fed by wind-blown
dune sand.

*Spartina* was deliberately planted at Foxton in **1913**; by Esler's survey it
covered more ground than any native community in the estuary. Pre-European, that area
is bare mud and native rushland.

### 4.8 Buried shoreline

**Tōtara logs in advanced decay lie behind the fore dune along the entire coast**,
and up to a kilometre inland north of Foxton — water-borne, marking a shoreline of a
century or more ago, buried by the advancing fore dune and re-exposed behind it.

## 5. Cross-cutting — the disturbance regime

Pulled out separately because these are the levers the installation already has.

| Driver | Evidence in Esler | Deep-time reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Fire** | Suspected Māori burning at Mt Stewart, on the dry downlands, and through Tiritea ~1760; scrub over stumps on the Rongotea downlands | Pre-human, lightning fire is rare in NZ — so in the game's window fire should be **volcanic/drought-driven and rare**, not routine |
| **Gale** | 1936 gale halved emergents in Keebles Bush; ~320 wind days/yr | Constant, characteristic; strongest on the montane edge and the coast |
| **Flood / avulsion** | Shingle-dome flora reset every flood; river outfall periodically restricted | Continuous channel migration across the plains |
| **Sand drift** | Post-1850 drift 3.2 km inland after cattle killed the spinifex | Glacial-age equivalent: Koputaroa phase, far larger |
| **Drought** | 1969–70 drought damage at Keebles; <914 mm on the Rongotea downlands | Sets the forest/grassland margin on the dry NW downlands |
| **Water-table rise** | Drowned tōtara forest under peat; anticline growth at Whirokino | Tectonic damming — slow, local, already in the plan |
| **Tephra** | Not covered by Esler | Already in TEMANAWA_RESEARCH.md (Whakamaru, Ōruanui) |
| **Browsing** | Possum/deer/cattle collapse of rātā–kāmahi, 1907→1960s | **Has no pre-human analogue** — moa browsing is different in kind and must be researched separately |

## 6. What Esler doesn't cover — the gaps we must fill elsewhere

1. **Fauna. Entirely absent — this is a botany handbook.** The installation is a
   moa/predator sim, so this is the largest gap by far. Needs its own dive: Worthy &
   Holdaway, NZ Birds Online, coprolite and gizzard-content work on what moa actually
   ate, and the Te Ahu a Tūranga assemblage already noted in TEMANAWA_RESEARCH.md.
2. **Moa as an ecological force.** Divaricating shrub architecture, the "moa browse"
   hypothesis, and how a browsed pre-human forest differs from Esler's cattle-browsed
   remnants.
3. **Freshwater and estuarine fauna** — īnanga, tuna, shellfish beds.
4. **Spatially explicit potential vegetation.** Esler's hand-drawn map has a modern
   successor in national pre-human/potential vegetation layers (Leathwick; LENZ) and
   Horizons' regional mapping.
5. **Standardised ecosystem names.** Singers & Rogers (2014) gives codes and
   consistent naming — e.g. **WF8 kahikatea, pukatea forest**; **DN2 spinifex, pīngao
   grassland/sedgeland** — worth adopting so the doc lines up with current practice.
6. **Firm dates for Polynesian-era burning.** The Initial Burning Period after
   arrival (~1280 CE) converted dry lowland podocarp forest to bracken **within
   decades**; this is what Esler was seeing the tail of and couldn't date.
7. **Current condition, for contrast.** Roughly **18% of the Manawatū catchment
   remains under indigenous cover**, most of it in poor condition — useful if the
   installation ever wants a "then and now" beat.
