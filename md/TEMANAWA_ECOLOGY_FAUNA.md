# Te Manawa — Deep Dive 5: Fauna

Companion to `TEMANAWA_FAUNA.md` (the scored longlist), which this supersedes on
detail and on the recommendation. Ecology first; build implications marked `[BUILD]`.
**§8 is the shortlist.**

**Window now 345 ka → ~25.5 ka (Ōruanui).** That extension matters more for fauna than
for anything else, because it now takes in the **Last Glacial Maximum**. The question
stops being "what lived here" and becomes **"what lived through it"** — and unusually,
there are answers, because moa ancient DNA records exactly that.

---

## 1. The evidence base, and its shape

Four kinds of evidence, each with a bias worth knowing:

| Source | Gives us | Bias |
|---|---|---|
| **Subfossil bone** | presence, size, assemblage composition | survives only in caves, dunes, swamps and gully fills — so the record is a map of *taphonomy*, not of abundance |
| **Ancient DNA** | species ID, population structure, glacial refugia | needs well-preserved bone; skews to the last ~50 kyr |
| **Coprolites and gizzard contents** | diet, and what was dispersed | **~90% of identified samples are from just three South Island species.** North Island moa diets are poorly known |
| **Middens and raptor accumulations** | predator diet | biased to what a predator could carry to a roost |

`[BUILD]` The Manawatū's own taphonomy is **gully fills, tephra falls and dune ponds
burying bone** — which is precisely why the Te Ahu a Tūranga assemblage exists. The
same processes the installation renders are the reason there is anything to render.

## 2. The local assemblage — Te Ahu a Tūranga

The strongest local hook in the entire project, and it is in this building.

- Found **March 2021** during excavation at the western end of the highway alignment,
  in the Ruahine foothills.
- **Bones of at least two individual smaller moa**, from **two moa species**, plus
  other large flightless birds and smaller bird-bone fragments.
- Largest complete elements: a **tibia ~350 mm long** and **two right femurs of
  differing lengths** — the duplication is what establishes a minimum of two
  individuals.
- Also a **pelvis, vertebra and other leg bones**.
- Taken to **Te Manawa** for safekeeping; analysed by **Dr Richard Holdaway**, working
  with Te Manawa Head of Collections and Exhibitions **Jeff Fox** and Te Ahu a Tūranga
  Kaitiaki **Terry Hapi**.
- Dated to **at least 180 ka, possibly ~345 ka** — the older estimate sits almost on
  the Whakamaru eruption.
- The wider assemblage as recorded in `TEMANAWA_RESEARCH.md`: **two moa genera, an
  extinct predator, and the extinct North Island goose, with both hatchling and adult
  bones.** Returned to the Ruahine maunga under tikanga.

**Why this is worth more than its size.** It isn't a single bone — it's an
**assemblage**, with an age bracket that falls inside the installation's window,
recovered from the ground the installation depicts, and it includes **hatchlings**,
which means a breeding population rather than a wandering individual.

`[BUILD]` Everything in §8's shortlist that appears in this assemblage should be
treated as a priority. It is the difference between "moa lived in New Zealand" and
"these animals lived *here*, and we have them."

## 3. The moa — what they actually were

### 3.1 The four North Island species

| Species | Size | Habitat |
|---|---|---|
| **NI giant moa** *Dinornis novaezealandiae* | females of the genus stood to **3.6 m** — the tallest bird that ever lived | tall, high-rainfall forest |
| **Little bush moa** *Anomalopteryx didiformis* | **50–90 cm at the back, 26–64 kg** | lowland **closed-canopy** forest |
| **Mantell's moa** *Pachyornis geranoides* | the smallest NI moa | **drier forest and shrubland**, throughout the NI |
| **Coastal moa** *Euryapteryx* | ~80–90 kg | coastal, southern half of the NI |

**Extreme reverse sexual dimorphism.** *Dinornis* females were vastly larger than
males — so much so that the sexes were originally described as separate species, and
the genus was only resolved by DNA. `[BUILD]` **This is a free gift: two sprites, one
species, and a genuinely surprising fact.** A towering female beside a much smaller
male reads instantly and needs no caption.

### 3.2 Diet — browsers of the understorey

The best-resolved North Island-relevant picture comes from little bush moa coprolites
(Fiordland, mid-Holocene, the southernmost coprolite site known and the longest
accumulation — ~2,200 years in one place):

- A **browser of a diverse range of tree and shrub species in the forest understorey**.
- **Ground fern foliage was an important part of the diet** — a new and unexpected
  finding, and a possible role as a **disperser of ground fern spores**.
- **Red mistletoe** (*Peraxilla tetrapetala*) leaves — highly palatable, and today
  sought out by possums and deer for the same reason.
- Gizzards elsewhere show twigs, leaves and fruit.

### 3.3 The seed-dispersal paradox — a correction to Deep Dive 3

I assumed in Dive 3 that moa were significant seed dispersers. **They were not.**

- **Moa gizzards destroyed every seed larger than ~3.3 mm.**
- They ate the big seeds of *Prumnopitys* (mātai, miro) and *Elaeocarpus* (hīnau) —
  almost half of analysed gizzards contained them — but **did not pass them intact**.
- Moa dispersed **only the tiny seeds of low-growing herbs and shrubs**. Even the
  silvereye, New Zealand's smallest seed-dispersing bird, disperses larger seeds than
  the largest moa ever did.
- Little bush moa coprolites contained **very few seeds of any size**.

`[BUILD]` So the honest version: **kererū was always the large-seed disperser; moa were
large-seed destroyers.** That's a better story than the one I had — the megaherbivore
and the pigeon were doing opposite jobs on the same fruit — and it means the modern
dispersal collapse (Dive 3 §8.7) is a *kererū* problem, not a moa-shaped hole.

## 4. Living through the ice — what the extended window buys us

Now that the run reaches the LGM, this is the fauna story.

Ancient DNA across all nine moa species shows:

- **Populations were highly structured.** *Pachyornis geranoides*, *Dinornis
  novaezealandiae* and *D. robustus* held **the same geographic locations for hundreds
  of thousands of years.**
- **Glacial cycles fragmented them.** Upland moa carry four geographically distinct
  mitochondrial lineages, attributed to Pleistocene glaciations; heavy-footed moa show
  north/south structure from **contraction into separate glacial refugia**.
- **Species responded differently.** Some tracked the shifting distribution of their
  preferred habitat; others were **restricted to refugia** as that habitat contracted.
  Eastern moa were hit hardest at the LGM, contracting to a **single** southern refugium.

> `[BUILD]` **The moa show the same pattern the plants do.** Deep Dive 4 found
> forest surviving the glacial in sheltered micro-refugia rather than being wiped out
> and recolonising. The moa did exactly that too. **One rule — contract to shelter when
> cold, re-expand when warm — applied to both plants and animals.** That is the deep
> time story told twice, in two kingdoms, from one line of code.
>
> And it gives the cold phase a shape beyond "less of everything": populations don't
> vanish, they **clump** into the gullies and sheltered slopes, then spill back out.

## 5. The predator — kērangi in detail

Enough new detail here to change the predator model.

**Circus teauteensis** Forbes, 1892. Named for James Eyles. **The largest harrier
species that ever lived.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Weight** | **3.0–3.5 kg** — up to **four times** the swamp harrier; feet and talons ~50% larger |
| **Wingspan** | ~2 m; the size of a small golden eagle |
| **Build** | **the body structure of a giant goshawk** — compact wings, powerful long legs |
| **Senses** | skull structure indicates **highly directional, owl-like hearing** |
| **Hunting** | a **fast flyer capable of hunting flighted birds in forest** — not a soaring open-country hunter |
| **Habitat** | **dry forest and shrubland**, Wairarapa to Coromandel in the NI, **sea level to subalpine** |
| **Nesting** | prey remains suggest it nested at **rock shelters and cave entrances** |
| **Population** | *"unknown but probably sparse"* |
| **Geography** | **North Island birds markedly larger than South Island** — two species suspected. **Ours are the big ones.** |

**Diet, from a North Island midden site:** **kererū was the favoured prey**, with **NI
kōkako, kākā and Finsch's duck** also recovered. North Island birds were large enough
to take **moa up to 40 kg**.

**Why it lost and the swamp harrier won:** Eyles' harrier was **too heavy and
small-winged for the gliding and soaring needed to hunt over open grassland**. Once
humans burned the forest and shrubland off and replaced it with grass, the niche
inverted. Swamp harrier bones start appearing in the record about when Eyles' harrier's
stop.

### `[BUILD]` Three changes to the predator model

1. **It hunts *in* forest and forest edge, with fast dashes** — goshawk, not
   circling harrier. The current eagle behaviour probably soars; it shouldn't.
2. **Its prey is birds, with small and juvenile moa as a subset** — not adult giants.
   40 kg is the ceiling, which excludes an adult *Dinornis* entirely.
3. **It should be at home in the dry, open phases, not diminished by them.** "Dry
   forest and shrubland, sea level to subalpine" is the glacial-phase habitat. The
   received idea that cold = fewer predators is wrong here.

`[BUILD]` And one lovely coupling for free: **the harrier's favourite prey is the
kererū, which is also the only bird that disperses large fruit.** Predator, prey and
plant in a three-link chain, all sourced, all cheap.

## 6. The supporting cast that earns its place

| Animal | Why it's here |
|---|---|
| **North Island goose** *Cnemiornis gracilis* | **In the Te Ahu a Tūranga assemblage.** Large flightless grazer — open-country, so abundant in cold phases |
| **Kererū** | The harrier's favoured prey **and** the only large-seed disperser. Does two jobs and everyone knows it |
| **Finsch's duck** *Chenonetta finschi* | **Confirmed prey** in a North Island Eyles' harrier midden. Near-flightless grazer; one of the most abundant birds in pre-human New Zealand; open and shrubland country |
| **NI kōkako, kākā** | Also in that midden — so they're documented forest fauna of the right kind, if ambient birds are ever wanted |

**Still barred, and worth restating because both are correctness traps:** the **swamp
harrier** and **pūkeko** both arrived only after Polynesian settlement opened the
forest. Both are what a general audience pictures as New Zealand wetland birds. Both
would be wrong.

## 7. The cast across the cycle

`[BUILD]` This is the legend — how a visitor reads the vegetation without text.

| | **Warm (interglacial)** | **Cold (glacial / LGM)** |
|---|---|---|
| **NI giant moa** | abundant — tall forest | contracted to sheltered forest |
| **Little bush moa** | abundant — closed forest | **scarce**; closed canopy is gone |
| **Mantell's moa** | present in dry patches | **abundant** — dry forest and shrubland |
| **Coastal moa** | on the dune belt | follows the coast **~30 km west** |
| **NI goose** | scattered | **abundant** — open grazing |
| **Finsch's duck** | scattered | **abundant** — open and shrubland |
| **Kērangi** | forest hunter | **at home** — dry forest and shrubland is its habitat |
| **Kererū** | abundant | scarce |

**The point the table makes:** cold is **not** death. It is a **different, busier
assemblage of large grazing birds**, with the same apex predator perfectly comfortable
in it. That's a more interesting — and more accurate — thing to show than a landscape
emptying out.

### Scores highest, fits worst — the honest note on huia

> ⛔ **Resolved: huia is in the sim.** The recommendation below was withdrawn by
> `TEMANAWA_FAUNA_POOL.md` §0 and the decision is recorded in `TEMANAWA_PLAN_V2.md`
> §5.1 and §10.3. The argument below rests on "it will not read at sprite scale," and
> that premise is wrong: the sim is a **stylised bird's-eye cartoon**, so a huia sprite
> stands for huia in an area, not for an individual bird at literal scale. Its bill
> dimorphism gives the same free-second-sprite payoff as *Dinornis*. **The text below is
> kept as a record of the reasoning, not as live guidance.**

**Huia scores 5/5/5/5 — the only animal on the longlist that does.** 646 skins taken
from the forest between the Manawatū Gorge and Akitio in 1883; a rāhui placed on the
Tararua by the chiefs of the Manawatū and Wairarapa; last confirmed sighting in the
Tararua, 28 December 1907.

But: it is crow-sized, dark, and lives inside a forest canopy — it will not read at
sprite scale — and its story is a **colonisation-era** story, not a deep-time one. On
a screen set 345–25 ka it would be present but invisible and off-narrative.

`[BUILD]` **Recommendation: keep huia out of the sim and put it in the interpretation
around it.** It's the single strongest Manawatū animal story in the research and it
deserves better than being three pixels in a canopy.

### Cut, with reasons

Haast's eagle (South Island only); swamp harrier and pūkeko (both post-1280 CE — and
wrong in a way visitors will assume is right); tuatara (recognition high, Manawatū link
weak, unreadable at scale); long-tailed bat (arrived <2 Ma, span uncertain); adzebill
and mōho (high wonder, but competing with better-scoring flightless birds for the same
visual slot); the small forest birds (**one flock asset, not five entities**).

## 9. To verify before committing art

1. **Which predator is in the Te Ahu a Tūranga assemblage.** `TEMANAWA_RESEARCH.md`
   records "an extinct predator" without naming it. Confirming it is Eyles' harrier
   would lift its Local score to 5 and let the installation say *"this bird, from this
   ground."* **Ask Te Manawa and Dr Holdaway directly** — this is a phone call, not a
   literature search, and it's the highest-value open question in the project.
2. ***Euryapteryx* taxonomy** — *gravis* vs *curtus* remains unsettled, and the coastal
   southern-NI distribution attaches to *gravis*, which some authors sink into *curtus*.
3. **Species-level identity at 345 ka.** The aDNA population-structure result is
   encouraging but rests on late-Quaternary material. Keep labelling species as
   provisional at the old end of the window, as the plan already does.
4. **Whether *Dinornis* dimorphism is safe to show.** It is well established, but check
   current size estimates before drawing a 3.6 m female — recent work has revised
   several moa masses downward.
