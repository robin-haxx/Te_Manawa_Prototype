# Te Manawa — Deep Dive 1: The Coast and Dune Lands

Companion to `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY.md` §4. Ecology first; build implications marked
`[BUILD]`. Distilled spec at §10–12.

**Headline:** the Manawatū dunefield is the largest transgressive dunefield in New
Zealand (~900 km²), and the one landform on this coast whose *driver has not changed*
across the whole deep-time window. The sand's mineralogy shows the prevailing
north-westerly was blowing in the last glacial exactly as it blows now. Everything
else — sea level, forest, the coastline's position — oscillates. **The wind is the
constant.**

---

## 1. The sand machine

**Supply.** Sand arrives by littoral drift running south, carrying erosion debris from
the **Rangitīkei, Whangaehu and Whanganui** rivers — volcanic-derived, and therefore
carrying **magnetic minerals not available locally**. That signature is the whole
argument: Koputaroa dune sand is ~26–32% magnetics and near-identical to Holocene
dune sand, but completely unlike sand from local rivers (which is coarser, poorly
sorted, negatively skewed). So the dunes are built from **shelf and beach sand blown
inland**, not from braided riverbeds — overturning Cowie's and Fleming's original
reading (Shepherd 1985; Hawke & McConchie 2005).

**Rate.** Sand is so plentiful that the coast prograded **0.6–1.0 m/yr** in Esler's
day, with the foredune advancing the same distance. Since sea level stabilised
~6,500 yr ago the coast has built out ~3 km.

**Wind.** ~320 wind days a year at Palmerston North; **W and NW on nearly half of
them**; gales possible in any month — 4–5 days a month in winter with gusts over
64 km/h, 6–8 days a month the rest of the year. The wind also carries large amounts
of salt and desiccates hard. Prevailing NNW drives net longshore drift southward.

> **The deep-time payoff.** Because Koputaroa sand is mineralogically coastal, not
> fluvial, "the present prevailing north-westerly winds were also characteristic of
> the last glacial" (Hawke & McConchie 2005). `[BUILD]` **Wind direction is a fixed
> constant for the entire 345 ka → 50 ka run.** Dunes always march inland toward the
> ESE. That's a free, defensible piece of visual coherence — every dune in every era
> points the same way.

## 2. Dune phases — the revised chronology

Esler works with three phases. The standard framework since Cowie (1963) has four,
and the extra one sits inside our window.

| Phase | Current dating | Position | Esler |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Koputaroa** | Deposited **at or before 40–50 ka**; **remobilised ~24–10 ka** | 5–25 km inland, tens of m high, hundreds of ha | *absent* |
| **Foxton** | pre-Taupō Tephra, ~4,000–2,000 yr BP | inland belt, black/brown sands | 3rd phase |
| **Motuiti** | ~1,000–500 yr BP | middle belt | 2nd phase |
| **Waitārere** | from ~100 yr BP, still accumulating | active coastal strip | 1st phase |

**Koputaroa in detail** — this is the one that matters:

- Shepherd & Price (1990) TL-dated Koputaroa sand **immediately underlying the
  Aokautere Ash** (= Kawakawa Tephra, from the Ōruanui eruption) at **24,200 ± 3,700
  yr**.
- Duller (1996) ran **nine IRSL samples** across the field and got **46,000 to
  9,900 yr**. His reading: the dunes originally formed **during an interstadial early
  in the last glacial cycle (MIS 3), when the coastline was relatively close to the
  present dune position**, then were **remobilised around the time of the Kawakawa
  Tephra**.
- Pollen from peat within Koputaroa sand shows it formed under **cool conditions**
  (McIntyre 1963).
- At the LGM, sea level was 100–130 m lower and the shoreline sat **~30 km west of
  today's**.

**Important caveat.** Muckersie & Shepherd (1995) showed the phases are
**time-transgressive** — they are mapping units defined by soil development, not
synchronous events, and they overlap in time and vary along the coast. Hawke &
McConchie found the Otaki–Te Horo Koputaroa dunes stabilised ~10–11 ka, **13,000
years later** than their Manawatū counterparts, purely because of local sand supply.

`[BUILD]` So: a glacial-age, shelf-fed, NW-driven dunefield reaching 25 km inland,
building through MIS 3 and remobilising through the LGM. **This is precisely the
mechanism the terrain plan already assumes, and it is now dated and sourced.** The
Koputaroa episode also lands within a few tens of kyr of the Ōruanui eruption already
in `TEMANAWA_RESEARCH.md` — two independent events, one tephra marker bed.

## 3. The active foredune — three plants, three landforms

Esler's best work, and the most directly implementable finding in the whole book:
**each sand-binder builds a measurably different dune**, because each has a different
architecture and therefore a different effect on airflow.

| Species | Habit | Dune form | Height | Seaward slope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Spinifex** (kōwhangatara) | fast extensive rhizomes, diffuse flexible leaf cover | smooth, even, regular | ~6 m | **14–16°** |
| **Pīngao** (*Ficinia spiralis*) | long rhizomes, open tufts at intervals, stiff leaves | smooth, convex face, level top | **seldom >3 m** | **8–14°** |
| **Marram** *(introduced ~1890s)* | dense clumps | stepped, steep, blow-out prone | to ~8 m | **24–28°** |

**Mechanisms, in Esler's terms:**

- **Spinifex** calms wind evenly across a diffuse canopy so sand drops uniformly. Its
  rhizomes grow *down* slopes and into hollows, reaching the base of the foredune
  **where they prevent wave erosion**. As sand accumulates on the face it produces
  more rhizomes to permeate and stabilise it. It tolerates salt water well.
- **Pīngao** only *partially* traps sand — the open habit and slow rhizome elongation
  mean sand doesn't accumulate freely, so pīngao dunes stay low. Crucially, **it keeps
  the dune partly mobile** rather than locking it down. It tolerates salt water and
  grows down slopes, but once deflation starts it can't stop it.
- **Marram**, lacking the diffuse habit, causes heavy deposition in the lee of each
  clump; wind then funnels *between* clumps and cuts channels the roots can't heal.
  **Marram dunes are nearly always eroding dunes**, with blow-outs undermining turrets
  of sand.

Esler's verdict: spinifex appears to be **replacing** marram on this coast, because
marram is the inferior sand binder here.

> `[BUILD]` **Pre-European there is no marram.** The natural foredune is a
> spinifex/pīngao system: **lower, smoother, more mobile, more permeable to sand
> moving inland**. Every historic photograph of a big steep Manawatū foredune is
> showing you a post-1890s artefact. Build the low smooth one.

**Lee of the foredune** (Esler): spinifex, pīngao, **sand daphne (*Pimelea
arenaria*)**, **tauhinu (*Cassinia leptophylla*)** and **sand coprosma (*Coprosma
acerosa*)** — the first shrubs.

## 4. The parabolic dune machine

This is a proper procedural rule, not scenery.

**The floor.** Dry sand deflates down to **moist sand just above the water table**.
The water table under the plains **slopes evenly at about 2 m per kilometre**, and
that plane is the base level for the entire system — "one factor which imposes order
on this complex and seemingly disorderly topography."

**Vegetation inverts the arms.** On bare sand you get **barchan** dunes — crescents
with arms extending *forward*. Barchans are rare here. With even light vegetation the
arms are held back and trail *behind*, and the dune becomes **parabolic**. Parabolics
dominate the Manawatū and their trailing arms run **many kilometres**.

**The sequence:**

```
sand mound → parabolic dune, arms trailing → arms elongate → arms break away from the
foredune and advance inland (hundreds of metres in a dry summer) → the abandoned arm
becomes a longitudinal dune shared between two parabolics → adjacent plains coalesce
→ a large BASIN, bounded seaward by the foredune and landward by a REAR DUNE
(the coalesced apices)
```

**Geometry:** windward "wind sweep" is long, even and acts as a ramp; the slip face
sits at **36°** (angle of repose) and sand is pushed over it either on a broad front
or as a narrow tongue. Before final stabilisation the wind sweep steepens as sand
supply falls.

**Alignment:** the ~900 km² field is largely stabilised parabolics aligned **almost
exactly parallel with the coast**. `[BUILD]` Combined with the fixed NW wind, this
gives us a single dune-orientation rule good for the whole run.

## 5. The sand plains — a 50-year succession clock

Within a basin, new plains form in sequence behind the retreating rear dune. The
youngest is nearest the rear dune. Sand deflates to near the water table in summer
and a wet hollow forms. Then:

| Stage | Plants | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Bare wet hollow** | — | deflated to the water table |
| **1. Creeping turf** | *Myriophyllum votschii*, *Limosella lineata*, *Eleocharis neozelandica*, *Ranunculus acaulis*, **remuremu (*Selliera radicans*)** | tiny creeping natives; **exotics can't compete here** — they lack the rhizomatous habit and inundation tolerance |
| **2. Sedge fringe** | ***Carex pumila*** | spreads round the edge, gathers sand, **builds its own level mound ~0.3 m high** |
| **3. Mound colonists** | pīngao and (now) marram germinate on the moist margin and take the mound | |
| **4. Rushland** | **oioi (*Apodasmia similis*)** dominant, joined by *Schoenus nitens* | |
| **5. ~50 years** | **toetoe**, **tī kōuka (cabbage tree)**, sometimes **mānuka** and ***Olearia solandri*** | |
| **6. Drying out** | **knobby clubrush (*Ficinia nodosa*)** replaces oioi | oioi persisting on a dry plain = sand deposited recently |

Meanwhile the rear dune has moved another ~20 m away and a new depression has opened
behind it. The oldest plains, nearest the foredune, get **re-submerged in drifting
sand** and revert to low irregular dunes.

**Modern confirmation.** These dune-slack turf wetlands are now measured as genuinely
mobile: at Tangimoana, Foxton Beach and Himatangi they sit 200–400 m behind the
strandline, **migrate inland ~2.7 m/yr and wobble ~5.2 m/yr**. Esler intuited the
dynamism; it's now quantified.

`[BUILD]` This is a **conveyor belt**, not a climax succession. Plants don't win —
they get overrun, and the sequence restarts a few hundred metres inland. That is a
much better fit for an ambient sim than a standard succession tree.

## 6. Dune forest — the inland end state

Only about **2,100 ha** of forest on the whole sand country, almost all **>8 km from
the coast** and concentrated where the high second-phase (Motuiti) dunes spill over
the older third-phase (Foxton) sands.

**No single species characterises it — the combination does.** Physiognomic dominants:
**tawa, māhoe, tītoki, akeake (*Dodonaea viscosa*) and ngaio**, with appreciable
rewarewa, mapou, kaikōmako, hīnau, lancewood, pigeonwood, kōwhai, *Pittosporum
tenuifolium* and kānuka. Akeake and ngaio read as the coastal signature — though
Esler notes both also grow 40 km inland in the Manawatū Gorge, so they're not strictly
maritime.

**The pre-forest stage** is bracken and kānuka, into which lancewood, māhoe,
five-finger, wineberry, *Pittosporum tenuifolium* and cabbage tree establish freely —
**more abundantly on south-facing slopes**.

**Esler's unresolved question:** why didn't forest cover *all* the stable third-phase
dunes, when the habitat looks suitable? His answer, hedged: **fire**, probably
centuries before Europeans arrived. He can't prove it. Modern palynology says burning
after Polynesian arrival converted dry lowland vegetation to bracken within decades,
which fits — but that's ~1280 CE onward, far outside our window.

`[BUILD]` For the deep-time sim, **the forest/shrubland boundary on stable dunes is
set by climate and sand supply, not fire** — because there's no ignition source. Dune
forest should appear during long stable interglacial spells and be buried by advancing
sand rather than burnt.

## 7. Lagoons, estuary, and the buried shoreline

*(Full treatment belongs to Deep Dive 2 — wetlands. Summary only.)*

- **The lagoon chain.** Dunes moving inland dammed drainage off the gently sloping
  hinterland, producing lagoons ~3 km behind the beach — **Pukepuke, Koputara,
  Kaikokopu**, each with extensive swamp. Pukepuke is **less than 0.6 m deep**, with
  raupō rafting out from the margins.
- **Older generations underneath.** A compressed peat band **3.5–4.5 m below the
  surface** near Round Bush is a buried earlier lagoon. `[BUILD]` The lagoons are not
  permanent features — they're a *recurring* product of the dunefield, born and
  buried repeatedly.
- **Estuary.** *Juncus maritimus* var. *australiensis* and oioi in extensive sheets;
  *Sarcocornia*, *Triglochin striata* and *Scirpus americanus* on the mud fringe; and
  a distinctive **salt meadow of *Sarcocornia* growing on raised circular sand
  mounds**, reached only by the highest tides and fed by wind-blown dune sand — which
  Esler says resembles Farewell Spit more than anything else he knows.
- ***Spartina*** was planted at Foxton in **1913** and by 1970 covered more ground
  than any native community in the estuary. Exclude it entirely.
- **Buried shoreline.** **Tōtara logs in advanced decay lie behind the foredune along
  the entire coast**, and up to a kilometre inland north of Foxton — water-borne,
  marking a shoreline a century or more old, buried by the advancing foredune and
  re-exhumed behind it. `[BUILD]` A nice recurring visual: **the dunes eat forest and
  spit out the logs.**

## 8. What changes in a glacial — the extrapolation

The honest version of "extrapolate to deep time." Two states, oscillating:

| | **Glacial / cold stadial** | **Interglacial (the ~1840 picture)** |
|---|---|---|
| Sea level | −100 to −130 m | present-ish, or higher at peak |
| Shoreline | **~30 km west** of today | roughly today's, or inland of it |
| Dunefield | **Koputaroa mode** — shelf sand driven inland tens of km, tens of m high | Holocene mode — belts parallel to coast, progressively stabilising |
| Sand mobility | high; sparse cover, cool/dry/windy | lower; vegetation locks the older belts |
| Vegetation | open sand, turf, sedge, low shrubland | full sequence to dune forest inland |
| Wetlands | **abundant** — dammed drainage behind advancing dunes | lagoon chain, contracting as drainage matures |
| Wind | **north-westerly** | **north-westerly** |

**The interglacial extreme is worth building too.** During the Holocene transgression
the sea pushed *into* the lower Manawatū valley — an estuary reaching **east to
Shannon and north to Opiki**. `[BUILD]` At an interglacial highstand the "coast"
habitat isn't a strip at the map edge; it's a drowned river mouth pushing inland
across what is otherwise the lowland forest zone. That's a dramatic, sourced, and
cheap-to-render state change.

## 9. Fauna of the dunes — a warning

There is almost **no bird fauna specific to the dunes** in the sense the forest has
one. Dune animals are **invertebrates and shorebirds**:

- **Katipō** (*Latrodectus katipo*) — lives under driftwood and in open dune
  vegetation; **healthy populations at Himatangi and Foxton**; declines wherever
  marram, lupin or pines close the dune in. It is an **open-dune specialist**, which
  makes it a live indicator of exactly the pre-marram state we're building.
- **Sand scarab / mumutawa** (*Pericoptus truncatus*) — one of New Zealand's largest
  beetles at ~4 cm, larvae in the sand, **3–5 year life cycle**.
- **Shorebirds and waders** — the estuary end. See `TEMANAWA_FAUNA.md`.

`[BUILD]` Don't expect the dunes to carry the creature load. **The dunes carry the
landform drama; the forest and wetland carry the animals.**

## 10. `[BUILD]` Wind and the Storm button — spec

Your instinct is right, and the research supports going further than "a bit."

**Principle: wind is not an event, it's the background field. Storm is an amplitude
spike on a vector that is always on.**

**A. Baseline (always running)**

- Fixed direction: **north-west**, for the entire run. No exceptions, no era changes.
- A slow `sandFlux` term that advances the dune field toward the ESE at a rate set by
  `(windStrength × exposedSandFraction)`.
- `exposedSandFraction` is the key coupling: **vegetation cover reduces sand flux, and
  sand flux buries vegetation.** That feedback is the whole dune system in one line.

**B. Storm (the button)**

Rather than a generic "stall the predators" effect, a storm should do five things, all
sourced:

1. **Advance the dune field a step.** One storm ≈ one visible increment of the
   parabolic dune's inland march. Esler: arms advance *"several hundred metres in a
   dry summer."*
2. **Open blow-outs where cover is thin.** Below a cover threshold, punch a deflation
   hollow down to the water-table plane (which slopes 2 m/km — already a usable
   surface).
3. **Bury the lee.** Deposit at the slip face; anything short and non-rhizomatous in
   the lee gets buried.
4. **Salt-burn the seaward margin.** Esler stresses salt-laden desiccating wind as a
   plant filter independent of sand.
5. **Inland, snap emergents.** The 1936 gale halved the emergent trees in Keebles
   Bush. Storms should thin tall podocarps in the forest zone — the same button, a
   different effect by habitat.

**C. Species response to burial — the asymmetry that makes it readable**

This is what makes storms *change what you see* rather than just shaking the screen:

| Response | Species | Effect of a storm |
|---|---|---|
| **Thrives on burial** | spinifex, pīngao | **grow**; spread; dune rebuilds around them |
| **Tolerates** | *Carex pumila*, oioi, knobby clubrush | survive, no gain |
| **Buried out** | toetoe, tī kōuka, mānuka, *Olearia solandri* | knocked back to stage 3–4 |
| **Killed** | dune forest species (tawa, ngaio, akeake) | die; leave logs |

So: **a storm winds the succession clock backwards, and it does so unevenly.** Repeated
storms hold the plain at rushland; a long quiet spell lets it run all the way to dune
forest, which the next big storm sequence then buries — leaving the exposed logs Esler
found behind the foredune. That's a complete, self-closing loop with no scripting.

**D. Wind and the glacial cycle**

Tie `windStrength` baseline to the glacial index already in `TEMANAWA_TERRAIN_PLAN.md`
— colder and drier means stronger, more effective wind *and* thinner vegetation, so
sand flux rises non-linearly into a glacial. The dunefield should visibly surge inland
as the sim cools, and stabilise and green over as it warms. **Direction never changes.**

**Deliberate exclusions and why:**

- **Marram** — introduced ~1890s. Wrong for every era we render.
- ***Spartina*** — planted 1913.
- **Tree lupin** — introduced; Esler's ubiquitous dune shrub is a modern artefact.
- **Māhoe, tītoki, mapou, lancewood** — ecologically correct, visually generic. Fold
  into a single "broadleaf understorey" filler asset if one is needed at all.
- **Pimelea arenaria, tauhinu, the slack turf herbs** — real and important, but too
  small to read. Represent as a **ground-cover texture on the sand**, not as entities.

## 12. `[BUILD]` Six things to change or add

1. **Fix the wind direction to NW for the whole run.** Sourced, defensible, and it
   makes every dune in every era point the same way.
2. **Add a `sandFlux` field coupled to vegetation cover**, both directions. This is
   the dune system; everything else is presentation.
3. **Make the water table a plane sloping 2 m/km**, and let deflation cut down to it.
   Cheap, and it generates the wet hollows for free.
4. **Give the Storm button five habitat-dependent effects** (§10B) instead of one, and
   **make plant response to burial asymmetric** (§10C). This is where the storm earns
   its place.
5. **Add the Koputaroa state** — a glacial dunefield reaching ~25 km inland, active
   ~46–10 ka, sourced from a shelf 30 km west. It sits at the young end of the sim's
   window and it is the coastal expression of the glacial index already planned.
6. **Add the drowned-valley interglacial state** — estuary reaching to Shannon and
   Opiki. Currently the coast is a strip at the edge of the map; at a highstand it
   should reach into the middle of it.
