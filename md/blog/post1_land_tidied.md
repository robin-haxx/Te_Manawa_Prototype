This is the first of three posts that will detail the natural history research underlying *A River
Older Than Mountains*, an adaptation of the Avian Age framework for Te Manawa Museum in Palmerston
North.

This installation tells the story of the Manawatū River, a grand, winding waterway around which the
district has defined itself since the “earliest recorded references” (Knight, 2014, p. 17). As well
as being a fixture in the lives of the region’s inhabitants, the river is a geological marvel that
has played “a central role in determining how the region’s environmental history has unfolded”
(Knight, 2014, p. 24).

Long ago, the strait that separated the North and South Islands crossed the Manawatū region, and
over time it made its way south to become the Cook Strait we know. The Whanganui sea basin submerged
far more of the lower North Island (Rees et al., 2018), the North Island itself being a far more
recently risen landmass, as previous posts here have discussed.

The Wellington “greywacke block” (the stone underlying the land) to the south of the Manawatū also
kept a consistent connection to the upper South Island (Trewick & Bland, 2012). Each glacial locked
water up in ice and dropped the sea by as much as 100–130 m, moving the coast west across the shelf;
each interglacial brought it most of the way back, and in the warmest a few metres above today’s
(Spratt & Lisiecki, 2016). During these glacial “lowstands”, especially after 1 Ma (Figure 2),
“fingers” of land from Wellington to the northern Manawatū most likely formed continuous terrain, and
less often so did the western margin of the basin, from what is now Farewell Spit to Taranaki. The
deepest lowstands were brief: in the last glaciation the sea stood below about −100 m for only some
ten thousand years (Lambeck et al., 2014).

It was during these glacial times, before the Cook Strait cut the connection 0.5 million years ago
(Lewis et al., 1994, as cited in Trewick & Bland, 2012), that species of Te Waipounamu could migrate
to the North Island. The kiwi is one. Genomic dating puts the first kiwi colonisation of the North
Island from the south at 1.1–1.6 million years ago, and the split between the North Island brown
kiwi and its closest South Island relative, the rowi, at about 1.1 million years ago. Most of the
splits that made today’s kiwi lineages date to the major glacial advances of the last 800,000 years
(Weir et al., 2016).

We know so much about sea-level change over a million-year span because of the basin itself. It
holds up to 5 km of mostly marine sediment (Trewick & Bland, 2012), laid down in layers that track
global sea level glacial by glacial, and the cliffs at Castlecliff, near Whanganui, are one of the
world’s best records of sea-level change (Abbott & Carter, 1999).

## Rangitāne stories

Te Āpiti, the Manawatū Gorge, is perhaps the place most emblematic of people’s connection to the
river. Midway between the Ruahine and Tararua ranges, the river has held its level, carving a steep
passageway.

Rangitāne hold the gorge as an ancestral passage, and it has many tapu features, such as Ngā Whatu,
“the brothers”. Tradition says the gorge was formed by the great tōtara Ōkatia, who, caught against
the ranges, forced his way through to the sea (Kawana, 2023; McKinnon, 2015b). The gorge also gave
Rangitāne a stronghold, with protection and strategic advantage. A song in the tribe’s history
begins, “Pleasant is the gorge at Te Rau-a-moa” (McEwen, 1986, p. 221).

## The river it became

At around 2 Ma, the Manawatū was a proto-river that flowed east, toward Hawke’s Bay, rather than
west: a remnant of a connection that spanned the entire landmass. Unbeknownst to it, this river would
be redirected and given new life, fed by tributaries and finding a new source in the peaks that rose
around it in slow, seismic shifts (Trewick & Bland, 2012).

The process that raised the Tararua and Ruahine ranges, which the river carved its way through, is
plate subduction, expressed as movement on the Wellington, Wairarapa and Ruahine faults (Trewick &
Bland, 2012). The Pacific Plate made a low dive beneath the Australian Plate, which was pushed up: a
less drastic effect than the Southern Alps, but one that has sped up over time (McKinnon, 2015a).

The volcanoes help to date this. Up to about a million years ago, ignimbrite from eruptions at the
Mangakino volcanic centre, north-west of today’s Lake Taupō, flowed east across the line where the
ranges now stand and into Hawke’s Bay, so the ranges cannot yet have been much of a barrier (Trewick
& Bland, 2012). Among these was the Kidnappers eruption, about a million years ago.

As the ranges began to rise, they held the young river back. From about 1 Ma, uplift on the western
side of the Wellington Fault near the gorge ponded a lake behind it, which later drained west toward
the Tasman Sea (Trewick & Bland, 2012), an echo of Ōkatia caught against the mountain until he carved
his way through Te Āpiti to the sea. The river would have carried volcanic sediment to the coast.
The shell bed that the reconstruction I am citing is built around, the Waitapu Shell Conglomerate,
holds the first pumice from the Kaukatea eruption, about 0.9 Ma (Rees et al., 2019).

Volcanic terrain is the other thread in this story. The Taupō Volcanic Zone has erupted on a vast
scale throughout the last million years, spreading ignimbrite and ash across the central North
Island. Trewick and Bland (2012) count its eruptions, along with climate cycling and the sea’s
comings and goings, among the forces that have periodically destroyed local life here, though
because these events often coincided, their effects are hard to tell apart.

*A River Older Than Mountains* features four of these eruptions as major markers on its timeline,
doubling as attention-grabbing “resets”. They don’t destroy entire forests, as they doubtless did
closer to Taupō, but they offer a chance to teach the succession of pioneer plants such as mānuka
and scrub, which took centre stage at these moments.

Over time, volcanic sediment was blown inland from the coast, creating the region’s characteristic
dune fields. In places the Koputaroa sand lies just beneath ash from the Ōruanui eruption at Taupō
(Shepherd & Price, 1990). That eruption came about 25,500 years ago, close to the coldest point of
the last glaciation (Vandergoes et al., 2013), and spread ash from Taranaki to the Chatham Islands
(Trewick & Bland, 2012). It is the last of the four eruptions on the installation’s timeline, and
where the timeline ends.

Early versions of *A River Older Than Mountains* showed the change from the far more sea-dominated
landscape of the Manawatū Strait. The habitat simulation now runs from 1 million years ago, stopping
to show how the sea rose and fell between glacial and interglacial periods. Modelling the land’s
shape over time is in many ways speculative. That lets me set the parameters of what we know, such
as roughly when more land was exposed and where we think the ranges and sea basins created higher
ground, and leave the exact shape to random generation, so the experience is slightly different
every time.

## The river today

The river finds many paths. Its source is now north of Norsewood, east of the Ruahine Range
(McKinnon, 2015a). After passing through the ranges, it receives the Pohangina River and veers
south. From here it has two distinct phases:

1. To Ōpiki it follows a high gradient, carrying gravel in a fairly straight, gravel-bedded channel,
   driven by the momentum it builds. In the cold periods the ranges were stripped bare and the
   river, choked with gravel, built its bed up into terraces; in the warm periods forest clothed the
   ranges, the gravel supply fell away, and the river cut down again (Knight, 2014, p. 20). “The
   youngest suite of terraces closest to the river represents variations in the river route during
   the current postglacial period” (Knight, 2014, p. 20).
2. From Ōpiki it has a gentler gradient and a less direct path. It can no longer carry gravel and
   runs muddy and silty, with many abandoned meanders and cut-off lagoons. Its alluvium built
   wetlands, basins and levées, and its overflow formed swamps (Knight, 2014, p. 20). Pā were later
   established in the area.

From there it reaches the sea at Foxton Beach.

The lower river built ancient wetlands. Levées formed along its banks, swamps ponded behind them and
peat accumulated; the ground subsided, and in time the river broke out to a new course (Esler,
1978). The Taonui basin lies between the levées of the Manawatū and the Ōroua. Before drainage, the
region held 264,511 ha of wetland; under 3% remains, at 6,983 ha (Forest & Bird, 2018). I will cover
these habitats in a later post.

You may think that the Manawatū’s prehistoric land connection would make it a hub for biodiversity.
It was indeed pivotal in our native species’ journey between north and south, but its relatively
short time above sea level means the region has less endemism. Most species arrived from the north:
despite the often hostile volcanic terrain, there were more continuous and varied routes for flora,
fauna and insects to establish. This is a basic impression of how changing land formed the
Manawatū’s less endemic pocket of Aotearoa, drawn mostly from Trewick and Bland (2012). I will
expand on it in the next post, where we return to *The Lost World of the Moa* (Worthy & Holdaway,
2002) to learn about Eyles’ harrier.
