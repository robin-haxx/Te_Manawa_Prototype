## Introduction

This is the first of three posts that will detail the natural history research underlying *A River
Older Than Mountains*, an adaptation of the Avian Age framework for Te Manawa Museum in Palmerston
North.
This installation tells the story of the Manawatu river, the fixture of the district “has been
defined in ways centered around [since] earliest recorded references” (Knight, 2014, p. 17) for its
inhabitants, but is also a unique geological feature that has played “a central role in determining
how the region’s environmental history has unfolded” (Knight, 2014, p. 24).

## Before the river

Long ago, the strait that separated the North and South islands crossed the Manawatu region, and
made its way south to create the Cook Strait we know. The Whanganui sea basin submerged far more of
the lower North Island; the North Island itself being a far more recently risen landmass since the
Oligocene, as previous posts here have discussed (Rees et al., 2018).

> **Figure 1.** The lower North Island at 2.7 Ma (Mangapanian), showing the Whanganui Basin and the
> Manawatū, Ruataniwha and Kuripapango straits. From Rees et al. (2018). *[C1]*

Additionally, the Wellington “greywacke block” (stone underlying the region of land) to the south of
the Manawatu had a consistent connection to the upper South Island (Trewick & Bland, 2012).
During glacial “lowstands,” especially after 1 Ma (fig. 1) *[C2]* where sea level fell by 100+
metres, is when these “fingers” of land from Wellington to the northern Manawatu would most likely
have formed continuous terrain, as well as less frequently on the Western margin of the basin from
what is now Farewell Spit to Taranaki. It is during these glacial times, before the Cook Strait cut
off the connection 0.5 million years ago (Lewis et al., 1994, as cited in Trewick & Bland, 2012),
that the deeply endemic species of Te Waipounamu could make migrations to the north island, creating
the north island species of these birds. *[C3]*

That rise and fall of the sea runs through everything that follows. Each glacial locked water up in
ice and dropped the sea by as much as 100–130 m, moving the coast west across the shelf; each
interglacial brought it most of the way back, and in the warmest a few metres above today’s (Spratt &
Lisiecki, 2016). The deepest lowstands were brief: in the last glaciation the sea stood below about −100 m for
only some ten thousand years (Lambeck et al., 2014), so a map of a lowstand coast shows a moment,
not an age. The land was moving too. Before about 1 Ma, the same fall in sea level bared far less of
the Whanganui Basin, because uplift had not yet raised the basin floor to meet it (Trewick & Bland,
2012).

We know this in such detail because of the basin itself. It holds up to 5 km of mostly marine
sediment (Trewick & Bland, 2012), laid down in layers that track global sea level glacial by
glacial, and the cliffs at Castlecliff, near Whanganui, are a reference section for the middle
Pleistocene (Abbott & Carter, 1999). *[C4]*

So if this was such a pivotal part of the terrain of ancient Aotearoa, why are there relatively few
species found only here?

The majority of migrations however came from the North, as despite the often hostile volcanic
terrain, there was more continuous and varied in-roads for flora, fauna and insects to establish.
This is a basic impression of how changing land formed the Manawatu’s rather less endemic pocket of
Aotearoa, mostly sourced from Trewick and Bland (2012), that sets the scene for the content of part 2
and 3 of this post.

That volcanic terrain is the other constant in this story. The Taupō Volcanic Zone has erupted on a
vast scale throughout the last million years, spreading ignimbrite and ash across the central North
Island, and Trewick and Bland (2012) count its eruptions, with climate cycling and the sea’s comings
and goings, among the forces that have periodically destroyed local life here. Their caveat is
worth keeping: all three changed at the same time, so their effects are hard to tell apart. Four of
these eruptions mark the installation’s timeline, and they appear below where they fall.

These figures illustrate their conception of how the land changed over time; using the
“interglacial” phases where sea level would have been at its maximum, with much less exposed land.
*[C5]*

## The river it became

So, at around 2 Ma, there was a proto-river that flowed east rather than west, toward Hawke’s Bay, a
remnant of a connection that spanned across the entire landmass, and unbeknownst to it, it would be
redirected and given new life, fed by tributaries and find a new point of origin in the peaks of
land that would rise around it in slow, seismic shifts (Trewick & Bland, 2012).

The gradual process that formed the Tararua and Ruahine Ranges that the river carved its way through
is Plate Subduction. *[C7]*

The Pacific plate made a low dive beneath the Australian plate, which was pushed up; a less drastic
effect than the Southern Alps, but a process which gradually sped up (McKinnon, 2015a). This is
evidenced by the gorge’s steeper incline in the lower, newer exposed rock. *[C8]*

The volcanoes help to date this. Up to about a million years ago, ignimbrite from eruptions at the
Mangakino volcanic centre, north-west of today’s Lake Taupō, flowed east across the line where the
ranges now stand and into Hawke’s Bay, so the ranges cannot yet have been much of a barrier (Trewick
& Bland, 2012). Among them, the Kidnappers eruption, about a million years ago, sent pumice into the
sea. *[C9]*

As the ranges began to rise, they held the young river back. From about 1 Ma, uplift on the western
side of the Wellington Fault near the gorge ponded a lake behind it, which later drained west toward
the Tasman Sea (Trewick & Bland, 2012). A reconstruction of the basin at about 0.88 Ma (Figure 3)
shows the lake filling behind a “bottle neck at gorge”, and catches the rest of this story at work:
the sea coming back across the plain after a glacial low, rivers avulsing and migrating across the
coastal plain, and volcanic sediment reaching the coast. The shell bed the reconstruction is built
around, the Waitapu Shell Conglomerate, holds the first pumice from the minor Kaukatea eruption,
about 0.9 Ma (Rees et al., 2019). *[C10]*

> **Figure 3.** The eastern Whanganui Basin at c. 0.88 Ma, with the “infilling of lacustrine basin –
> bottle neck at gorge” at lower right. From Rees et al. (2019). *[C1]*

While the river being an ancient fixture is not disputed, there are two theories as to how its
origin played out; Antecedence, the river forming along its current line (McKinnon, 2015a),
Or a period of “capture” at ~500 ka that set the river on its current course (Craw et al., 2019).
*[C11]*

On either reading, the river was on its present line by the time of the largest eruption in this
story. Around 340,000 years ago came the Whakamaru eruptions (Trewick & Bland, 2012), and their ash,
the Rangitawa Tephra, is a dated marker layer across the southern North Island (Kohn et al., 1992).
*[C12]*

## Te Āpiti

Te Apiti, the Manawatu gorge, is perhaps the area most emblematic of peoples’ connection to the river
itself. In the bed at the midpoint of these two ranges, the river has held its level, carving a steep
passage-way.

Rangitane hold the significance of the gorge as an ancestral passage, and it has many fixtures that
are tapu such as Nga Whatu / “The brothers”. *[C13]*

Tradition says it is the great totara Okatia forcing its way to the sea that formed the gorge
(Kawana, 2023; McKinnon, 2015b).

The river gorge created a stronghold that protected and provided strategic advantage to the
rangitane.

Song No. 29 (p. 221): “Pleasant is the gorge at Te Rau-a-moa”. *[C14]*

## The river today

The river finds many paths. Its origin now is north of Norsewood, east of the Ruahines (McKinnon,
2015a). After moving through the ranges, it receives the pohangina river and veers south.
From here, it has two distinctive “phases”:

1. To Opiki it follows a high gradient, carrying a flow of gravel and forming a rather straight
   channel with a gravel bed due to the momentum it builds. It aggraded terraces each glacial cycle,
   downcutting each interglacial, to build up how it looks in this phase (Knight, 2014, p. 20). In
   the cold periods the ranges were stripped bare and the river was choked with gravel, so it built
   its bed up; in the warm periods forest clothed the ranges, the gravel supply fell away, and the
   river cut down again. *[C15]*

2. From Opiki it has a smaller gradient, and follows a less direct path. It cannot carry gravel,
   rather being muddy/silty. It has many “abandoned meanders” and cut-off lagoons, alluvium created
   wetland, basins, levees, overflow forming swamps. (pa sites were later established in area) “The
   youngest suite of terraces closest to the river represents variations in the river route during
   the current postglacial period” (Knight, 2014, p. 20). *[C16]*

From here it finds its way to the sea via Foxton beach.

Where it meets the sea has never stayed put. At each glacial lowstand the coast lay out to the west,
across what is now seabed, and the river ran out to meet it; at each interglacial the sea came
back across the plain. In the last interglacial, about 125,000 years ago, the sea stood a few metres
above today’s and cut the Tokomaru marine bench along the foot of the ranges (the brown area at top
right of Figure 4). After the last glaciation, the rising sea backed up the valley as an estuary
reaching Shannon and Opiki (Clement et al., 2010). *[C17]*

> **Figure 4.** Meanders: LiDAR of the Manawatū River from Opiki to Longburn, showing its historic
> courses. The brown area at top right is the ancient Tokomaru marine bench. From Knight (2014,
> Fig. 3); LiDAR image by A. Steffert, Horizons Regional Council. *[C1]*

Dunes – broad strokes here. This needs a bit more research.
The glacial-age Koputaroa dune sand carries the same coastal, volcanic-derived magnetic minerals as
today’s beach sand. So the north-westerly blew in the last glaciation exactly as it blows now (Hawke
& McConchie, 2005). In places the Koputaroa sand lies just beneath ash from the Ōruanui eruption at
Taupō (Shepherd & Price, 1990). That eruption came about 25,500 years ago, close to the coldest point
of the last glaciation (Vandergoes et al., 2013), and spread ash from Taranaki to the Chatham Islands
(Trewick & Bland, 2012). It is the last of the four eruptions, and where the installation’s timeline
ends. *[C18]*

## Habitats overview

Brief overview of habitats this creates without going into detail

The river created the ancient wetlands.
Uplift steepens the catchment and feeds sediment down the gorge: shingle as far as Palmerston North,
fine silt below. The river builds levées along its banks, swamps pond behind them, peat accumulates,
the ground subsides, and the river eventually avulses to a new course. The Taonui basin is a basin
formed inside the levées of the Manawatū and the Ōroua. *[C20]* Before drainage, the region held

## Sim notes

- **S1. What the sim shows.** The run opens at 1 Ma, on Trewick and Bland's Fig. 4D, and closes at
  25.5 ka on Ōruanui. *Before the river* and the 2 Ma proto-river are backstory the sim doesn't show;
  say so plainly in *Adapted*.
- **S2. "Over the course, this is the sequence of terrain."** As of 2026-10-19:
  - **~1 Ma.** Low hills along the axis. The east is land. The river already flows west through the
    gap, with an estuary arm to the west and the sea deepest off Levin–Foxton.
  - **~0.9 Ma.** The Woodville lake ponds behind the gorge (full ~0.92–0.8 Ma) and drains by ~0.6 Ma.
    That matches the 0.88 Ma map's "infilling of lacustrine basin".
  - **By ~0.5 Ma.** The south and west have emerged.
  - **Throughout.** The coast follows relative sea level in metres, glacial by glacial. Highstands
    flood the plain toward the range foot and lowstands bare it, with the plain's slow uplift counted
    in. The sim's coast matches the 0.88 Ma map as the sea rises out of the 880 ka low toward the
    835 ka high.
  - **Since 2026-10-19.**
    - The ranges are rugged, dissected country.
    - The lower river and the tributaries' lower reaches meander and migrate between peaks, and run
      straighter in glacials. This echoes Figure 3's "avulsion and migration of river channels" and
      Knight's abandoned meanders. Licence: the migration is smooth, with no cut-off oxbows or sudden
      avulsions.
    - Te Āpiti is a narrow, steep-walled, shingle-banked gorge with the ranges sloping in to meet it,
      deepening as the run goes on.
- **S3. The gorge rate.** At Barney's Point, uplift and river erosion both run at about 1.5 mm a year
  (Te Āpiti – Manawatū Gorge, n.d.). In the sim, incision runs slightly ahead of uplift, so the river
  is never defeated. The gorge is ~300 m deep, roughly 200,000 years of cutting at today's rate (a
  possible line for *Te Āpiti*).
- **S4. The sea and the plant boost.** A plant boost on the second screen runs a time-lapse to the
  next glacial/interglacial turn. At warm highstands the sea backs up the valley as an estuary: the
  Shannon–Opiki estuary in *The river today*, which ties this back to Clement et al. and Knight.
- **S5. The four eruptions in the sim:**

  | Eruption | Sim date | Notes |
  |---|---|---|
  | Kidnappers | 1 Ma | |
  | Kaukatea | 0.9 Ma | minor |
  | Rangitawa / Whakamaru | 349 ka | the catastrophic tier |
