# Te Manawa — Deep Dive 3: Lowland Podocarp Forest and the River Corridor

Companion to `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY.md` §2.2–2.4 and §2.6. Ecology first; build
implications marked `[BUILD]`. **§8 is the then-and-now context you asked for** — not
for the installation, for you.

**Headline:** this is where Esler had the least to work with — *"not a vestige remains
as it was a century ago"* — and where post-1980 research has done the most to fill the
gap. Two independent modern lines now corroborate and *explain* what he could only
infer from stumps and interviews: environmental modelling reproduces his forest map
from first principles, and podocarp regeneration ecology explains the one thing he
noticed but couldn't account for.

---

## 1. What Esler actually had

Worth being blunt about, because it sets how much weight the modern work carries.

For **half the district** — the forest that covered the plains and terraces — the
contemporary written record amounts to roughly two sentences:

- J. T. Stewart's survey map (S.O. 10603), inscribed across the entire plains:
  ***"Flat land. Totara matai, tawa and other timber."*** Esler: *"This seems to be
  the most detailed and, in fact, the only description of the early forest available
  to me."*
- A surveyor's line on the Linton terraces, 1868: ***"Fine level land. Rimu and tawa
  predominate but there is some secondary forest in the gullies."***

Everything else is stumps, logs, place names, and interviews conducted in the 1960s
with men recalling their youth. Of the tōtara forest — *the* forest the district was
known for — he writes plainly: **"Little can be written… because not a vestige remains
as it was a century ago."**

`[BUILD]` So of the three habitat groups, this is the one where we lean hardest on
modern inference. Fortunately it's also the one where modern inference is strongest.

---

## 2. Modern modelling reproduces his map — from a completely different direction

Leathwick's **Potential Vegetation Pattern** predicts New Zealand's pre-human forest
composition by regressing the distribution of major canopy tree species against
environment: annual and seasonal temperature, solar radiation, **soil and atmospheric
water deficit**, soil leaching, slope, soil parent material and drainage. No history,
no survey plans, no stumps — just species-environment relationships projected onto the
landscape.

**For the Manawatū plains it predicts *mataī–kahikatea–tōtara forest* and
*kahikatea–tōtara forest*.**

That is Esler's reconstruction, arrived at by a method that shares none of his
evidence. `[BUILD]` **Two independent methods agreeing is about as good as this
evidence ever gets.** Build mataī–kahikatea–tōtara on the plains and
kahikatea–tōtara on the wetter ground with real confidence.

**One limit to carry.** Potential vegetation models predict mature vegetation in the
absence of humans **using present-day species–environment relationships** — so they
assume today's climate. They do not transfer to a glacial. For cold phases the model
gives you the *method* (drive composition off water deficit, temperature and drainage)
but not the *answer*; that's what the glacial index in `TEMANAWA_TERRAIN_PLAN.md` is
for.

## 3. The gradient — three forest types, one axis

Wettest to driest, the lowland forest is a single drainage/moisture gradient:

| Position | Forest type | Dominants | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanently wet, fine sediment | **Kahikatea–pukatea semi-swamp** *(Deep Dive 2)* | kahikatea, pukatea | Taonui basin, back-swamps behind levées |
| Moist recent alluvium and terraces | **Mixed podocarp–broadleaf** | rimu, mātai, tōtara, kahikatea, miro **over tawa** | plains, terraces east of the river, Tiritea and Kahuterawa basins |
| Free-draining levées and terraces | **Tōtara forest** | tōtara, mātai, with miro, rimu, northern rātā | riparian levées; the Rongotea–Ohakea terraces |
| Droughty NW downlands | **Dry margin** | tōtara at the climatic edge, giving way to shrubland | Rongotea–Ohakea, <914 mm rain |
| Hills, gorge, older terraces | **Tawa forest** | tawa, near-pure in places | Manawatū Gorge, Tiritea, terraces |

### 3.1 The structure everyone gets wrong

From Keebles Bush, the only relic still holding numerous podocarps:

- **Emergent podocarps exceed 30 m**, diameters 0.3–1.5 m. Rimu most conspicuous,
  **mātai about as numerous**, then kahikatea, tōtara and miro.
- **The main canopy sits at only 9–15 m** — tawa and māhoe, with tītoki, kāmahi,
  rewarewa, hīnau, *Pittosporum tenuifolium*, mapou and lancewood.
- Kawakawa is the predominant shrub; 29 fern species; *Dicksonia squarrosa* on the
  narrow floodable stream terraces.
- **Podocarp regeneration negligible**, except a few saplings on steeper aspects.

> `[BUILD]` **Two layers, and the gap between them is huge.** Scattered giants at 30 m+
> over a continuous 9–15 m canopy. That silhouette is the single most important visual
> fact about this forest, and it is *not* what a modern remnant looks like (§8).

Rimu reaches ~50 m on good sites and lives **600–800 years, possibly to 1,200**;
tōtara to ~35 m. `[BUILD]` These trees are older than most of the events the
installation renders between them — an emergent rimu can stand through several
storms and a whole vegetation re-sort.

## 4. Why the podocarps weren't regenerating — the gap modern ecology filled

Esler noticed something and couldn't explain it:

> *"Over a span of many centuries the podocarps failed to produce sufficient numbers
> of surviving younger members to ensure a prominent place in the vegetation."*

He attributes the swing to tawa to two phases — **centuries of recruitment failure,
then a century of milling** — and leaves the first phase unexplained.

**The modern answer: podocarps don't regenerate continuously. They regenerate
catastrophically.** Even-aged cohorts establish after massive disturbance — fire, mass
movement, flooding, large-scale windthrow, or volcanic eruption. Ogden & Stewart's
'lozenge' model has a large distinct cohort following a major disturbance, succeeded
over time by smaller, less distinct cohorts. And explicitly: **the scarcity of
large-scale disturbance in old-growth podocarp–broadleaved forest is what produces the
podocarp regeneration gap.**

Species differ in how they respond. **Tōtara and kahikatea show the greatest
height-growth response to increased light**; mātai, rimu and miro respond less
(Ebbett & Ogden 1998).

> ### `[BUILD]` This is the most important mechanic in this document
>
> **Podocarps should not recruit continuously.** They should recruit in **pulses**,
> triggered by exactly the disturbances the installation already has:
>
> | Trigger | Already in the build |
> |---|---|
> | **Volcanic eruption** | Eruption button — and the sediment pulse at stage 5 *(Dive 2 §6)* |
> | **Large-scale windthrow** | Storm, forest behaviour *(Dive 1 §10; Dive 2 §7)* |
> | **Flooding / channel change** | Storm, lowland behaviour; the river model |
> | **Mass movement** | Uplift and steepening on the range flanks |
>
> Between pulses you get an **ageing, even-aged emergent cohort over a self-replacing
> tawa canopy** — which is precisely the Keebles Bush structure, and precisely the
> centuries-long trend Esler observed and couldn't name.
>
> Two refinements: give **tōtara and kahikatea a stronger light response** than mātai,
> rimu and miro, so a gap fills with a different mix depending on how big it is; and
> let cohorts **age out visibly**, so a long quiet stretch produces a thinning of
> giants rather than a static scene.

## 5. Tawa — the default state

Tawa is a component of every forest type except beech, and takes over wherever
podocarps are removed. In the Manawatū Gorge State Forest it forms a **pure canopy over
30 m** with almost no understorey — the closed tawa canopy is dark enough that even
māhoe and nīkau beneath it are struggling. The Tiritea reserve holds **the
best-preserved forest of any kind in the district**, protected by shelter from the
prevailing westerly.

`[BUILD]` **Tawa is the background; podocarps are the disturbance signal punched
through it.** If nothing happens for long enough, the map goes tawa. That gives the
buttons something to be *for*.

## 6. The dry margin — the one moisture-limited place on the map

The Rongotea–Ohakea terraces carried what was probably the largest tōtara block in the
district, in a place receiving **less than 914 mm of rain a year, subject to occasional
drought and fairly constant drying wind**. Esler's phrase: **"tōtara forest existed
near the climatic margin of forest and grassland."**

Edgar Dear's recollection, written for Esler shortly before his death: a line from
Rongotea cemetery toward Ōhakea held *"the finest stand of tōtara in the Manawatū"*,
with mātai, miro, rimu and **northern rātā** — *"some of the largest rātā that I have
ever seen… too large to be felled, killed by fire when the area was burnt. These trees
were a feature of the landscape for 25 years after the bush was felled."*

The "scrub country" the settlers found here — mānuka, toetoe, koromiko, tutu, bracken —
**stands over stumps.** It was recently burnt forest, not natural shrubland.

`[BUILD]` Two things follow:

1. **This is the one habitat where the limiting factor is moisture, not fertility** —
   which is why the Growth button should correctly do very little here *(Dive 2 §8)*.
2. **In a glacial this margin expands enormously**; in a warm interglacial it shrinks
   to a small north-western corner. It is the map's most climate-sensitive boundary and
   the best place to *show* the glacial index doing something.

## 7. Moa and the forest — and a Manawatū footnote

**Greenwood & Atkinson (1977)** proposed that New Zealand's divaricating shrubs —
**spineless, small-leaved, wide-angled and closely interlaced, making up nearly 10% of
the woody flora, a proportion apparently unmatched anywhere else** — evolved as
protection against moa browsing. The interlacing arises from reduced apical growth with
continued lateral growth; and tellingly, **some divaricates are the juvenile stages of
trees that lose the habit as they grow taller** — a height-escape signature.

**The Manawatū footnote:** R. M. Greenwood is the same botanist who appears throughout
Esler's handbook — Greenwood (1949) on Tōtara Reserve in the Pohangina, Esler &
Greenwood (1967, 1968), Atkinson & Greenwood (1972) on the drought damage at Keebles
Bush. The moa-browse hypothesis was co-authored by a botanist working this ground
alongside Esler. Worth knowing; not worth overclaiming.

**The debate is not settled.** At least three live hypotheses:

| Hypothesis | Proponents |
|---|---|
| Anti-moa browse defence | Greenwood & Atkinson 1977 |
| Microclimatic shield (frost, drought, wind) | McGlone & Webb 1981 |
| Light-harvesting / photoinhibition avoidance | Kelly 1994; Howell et al. 2002 |

Recent work on tensile strength, occurrence on remote moa-free islands, and the
interaction of macroclimate with topography keeps pulling in both directions. **Present
it as an open question**, not a settled fact — that's more interesting anyway, and it's
a genuine live argument about the very thing the installation is showing.

### 7.1 The line that matters most

From Greenwood & Atkinson's own summary:

> *"because moas fed in a different way from mammals there is little to support the
> idea that introduced browsing mammals have merely replaced moas as an ecological
> factor in New Zealand."*

`[BUILD]` **Moa browse is not deer-and-possum browse with a different sprite.** Moa
snip with a bill, from the ground to roughly head height, with no incisors, no
bark-stripping, and **no ability to climb into a canopy and defoliate it from above**.
That last is exactly what possums do — and it is why Esler's northern rātā–kāmahi
forest collapsed in three decades while the same forest stood under moa for millions of
years. Browsing pressure in the sim should shape **shrub architecture and the 0–3 m
layer**, and should have essentially **no effect on the canopy.**

Note the coastal and wetland flora shortlists already carry two divaricates — *Coprosma
acerosa* and mingimingi — so the browse-response form is already in the asset set.

## 8. Then and now — context for you

Not for the screen. For knowing what to point at.

### 8.1 Extent

| | Then | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Forest, Esler's survey area | **~half the district** | — |
| Manawatū Plains Ecological District | full cover | **under 5% indigenous vegetation** |
| Manawatū catchment overall | — | **~18%** (inflated by the ranges; the plains figure is the honest one) |
| Wetland, Manawatū–Whanganui | 264,511 ha | **6,983 ha — under 3%** |

Lowland loss is far worse than upland loss, for the obvious reason: the flat, fertile,
well-drained ground is the farmable ground.

### 8.2 The canopy is upside down

**Then:** scattered emergents above 30 m over a continuous 9–15 m broadleaf canopy.

**Now:** the emergents were the timber. They went first. So what a visitor calls "bush"
in the Manawatū today is **the understorey of the original forest with the roof taken
off.** Every remnant is a decapitated forest. This is the single most useful thing to
be able to say in front of a screen showing the real thing.

### 8.3 Tawa is over-represented — the forest lies about itself

Esler is explicit: remnants like Kitchener Park **"almost certainly have more tawa than
before milling."** Selective removal of podocarps promotes the species that was already
underneath. So the modern "tawa forest" of the terraces is frequently podocarp forest
with the podocarps subtracted — and it looks, misleadingly, like a natural type.

### 8.4 Northern rātā is functionally gone, and we watched it go

Possums were liberated near the Tiritea around **1907** and were numerous by the 1921
trapping season. Esler:

> *"The gaunt skeletons of northern rata in the Tiritea reserve when viewed from
> Palmerston North were a conspicuous feature of the skyline in the 1960s but towards
> the end of the decade most had fallen… There are not more than a dozen old living
> northern rata trees in the reserve today."*

Trees over 30 m stood on ridges that now carry closely-cropped turf. Hayes had walked
under a continuous canopy on that summit in 1927.

### 8.5 Kāmahi collapsed and left almost no evidence

Esler thinks kāmahi was a major canopy tree that the historical record simply missed —
settlers knew the timber trees and lumped kāmahi in as *"one of the others"*, and its
wood rots, so it barely appears in the stump record that documents tōtara and rātā so
well. `[BUILD]` A reminder that **the durability bias runs through everything**: the
forest we can reconstruct is biased toward the species that don't decay.

### 8.6 The browsing regime is categorically different

See §7.1. Moa browsed the shrub layer for millions of years. Possums, deer, goats,
pigs, sheep and cattle attack a different part of the plant, including the canopy from
above. **Introduced mammals did not step into a vacant niche — they opened a new one.**

### 8.7 Seed dispersal is broken

**Kererū is now the only widespread bird able to swallow and disperse the large fruit**
of tawa, taraire, karaka, miro and pūriri. Even where it persists:

- mean dispersal distances of about **95 m for tawa and 98 m for pūriri**
- **79–88% of seeds land within 100 m** of the parent
- **under 1% travel beyond 1,000 m**
- and introduced mammalian seed predators take much of what does land

So even in a remnant where the trees are alive, the **seed shadow is a fraction of
what it was**, and kererū numbers are declining. The forest may be standing and still
not be reproducing.

> **Refined in Deep Dive 5.** Moa never filled this role either — their gizzards
> destroyed every seed larger than ~3.3 mm, so they were large-seed *destroyers*, not
> dispersers. Kererū has **always** been the sole large-seed disperser. That makes this
> a kererū problem rather than a moa-shaped hole, and it makes the modern decline
> sharper, not softer. See `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY_FAUNA.md` §3.3.

### 8.8 The floor has been replaced

Esler documents the sequence: cattle enter, gaps fill with broad-leaved species, then
**Tradescantia forms a blanket over most of the floor and suppresses nearly all
regeneration** — *"this weed can spell doom to a remnant."* Then *Solanum
pseudocapsicum* and *S. diflorum*, which tolerate browsing and trampling that kills
native shrubs. *Clematis vitalba* is the significant alien climber.

His counter-intuitive line, from the Aokautere terraces: **"the richest floras are not
on the richest soils"** — fertile soils are captured by aliens fastest once disturbed.

### 8.9 The plains are drier, and the river is fixed

Drainage lowered the water table across the lowlands; peat subsided **a metre or more**
at Makerua, and the semi-swamp habitats that survive are drier than they were. And with
stopbanks, the Moutoa floodway and the cut-off Foxton loop, **the river no longer
avulses across its floodplain** — removing the very mechanism kahikatea needs to
recruit (Dive 2 §4.1), and the flood scour the shingle-bed flora depends on.

### 8.10 Where to go and look

| Place | What it is | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Keebles Bush** | the only relic with numerous podocarps; the reference stand for §3.1 | the 1936 gale halved the emergents; the 1969–70 drought did more |
| **Kitchener Park**, Feilding | landmark since settlement | grossly modified; native species count fell from **140 (1924) to about 80** by Esler's time |
| **Round Bush**, **Himatangi Bush Scientific Reserve** | semi-swamp and dune forest survivors | kahikatea felled ~1918 at Round Bush; Himatangi survives largely *because* this survey happened |
| **Bledisloe Park**, **the Esplanade** | kahikatea groups | too small to carry the character |
| **Tōtara Reserve**, Pohangina | Greenwood 1949 | Esler uses it as a **stand-in** — *"must serve as an account of riparian tōtara forest of the Manawatū"* — because nothing local survived |
| **Manawatū Gorge / Te Āpiti**, **Tiritea catchment** | best-preserved forest of any kind in the district | the tawa is real; the rātā–kāmahi above it is gone |

### 8.11 The one-sentence version

**What you see in a Manawatū bush remnant today is the 9–15 m understorey of a 30 m+
podocarp forest, standing on drained ground, with the rātā dead, the kāmahi
unrecorded, the tawa over-represented, the seed-dispersal network reduced to one
declining bird, and a browsing regime that attacks the canopy from above — something
nothing did here for sixty million years.**

both.

## 10. `[BUILD]` Six things to change or add

1. **Make podocarp recruitment episodic, not continuous** (§4). Tie it to eruption,
   windthrow, flood and mass movement. This is the mechanic that makes the whole
   deep-time forest behave correctly.
2. **Build the two-layer canopy** — scattered emergents above 30 m over a continuous
   9–15 m canopy (§3.1). Not a single canopy height.
3. **Make tawa the default state** the map decays toward (§5). It gives every
   disturbance button something to interrupt.
4. **Differentiate light response** — tōtara and kahikatea respond strongly to gaps,
   mātai/rimu/miro weakly — so gap size changes what fills it.
5. **Restrict browse to the 0–3 m layer** (§7.1). Moa should visibly shape shrub
   architecture and visibly *not* affect the canopy.
6. **Make the dry NW margin climate-driven** (§6) — the map's most responsive
   boundary, expanding through glacials and contracting through interglacials.
