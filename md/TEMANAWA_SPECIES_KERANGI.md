# Kērangi / Eyles' Harrier — Species Brief

***Circus teauteensis*** Forbes, 1892 · the apex predator of the installation.

Companion to `TEMANAWA_ECOLOGY_FAUNA.md` §5. This document exists to justify the
predator design notes properly, and because the taxonomy is a mess worth understanding
— you're right that it was unresolved when Worthy & Holdaway wrote *The Lost World of
the Moa* in 2002, and the resolution came afterwards.

---

## 1. In one paragraph

The largest harrier that has ever existed, and the only apex avian predator of the
North Island — Haast's eagle was confined to the South. It weighed **3–3.5 kg** with a
**~2 m wingspan**, had the **body of a giant goshawk** rather than a harrier, **owl-like
directional hearing**, and hunted **flighted birds inside forest** rather than soaring
over open ground. Its favourite prey was the **kererū**. It evolved to that size in New
Zealand **because the Pleistocene ice ages opened the landscape up** — and it was
finally undone when humans opened the landscape further, in a way it was no longer
built for.

---

## 2. The name — a genuinely convoluted history

This matters because you'll find it under two names in the literature, and the older
book uses the other one.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1800s** | **Augustus Hamilton** collects large harrier bones, probably from the **Te Aute region, Hawke's Bay — North Island** |
| **1892** | **Henry Ogg Forbes** names the material ***Circus teauteensis*** and ***Circus hamiltoni***. But the description is minimal and **no holotype was ever formally designated**, so both names are generally treated as **nomina nuda** |
| — | **Kálmán Lambrecht** later mentions a putative holotype tibiotarsus (still held at the BMNH) while explicitly stating Forbes' names were **invalid** |
| **1953** | **Ron Scarlett**, osteologist at Canterbury Museum, properly describes ***Circus eylesi*** from **Pyramid Valley — South Island**, naming it for **Jim Eyles**, palaeontologist and former director of the Nelson Provincial Museum and the West Coast Museum |
| **2002** | **Worthy & Holdaway** discuss the problem in *The Lost World of the Moa* without resolving it — hence the name you'll see in that book |
| **2022** | **OSNZ Checklist Committee**: ***C. teauteensis* is valid; *C. eylesi* is a junior synonym** |

**The reasoning for reinstating *teauteensis*** is that, as with many New Zealand birds,
it is reasonable to assume the North and South Island populations were **at least
distinct subspecies, if not distinct species** — so the North Island material deserves
its own name.

There's also a loose end: harrier bones in **Walter Mantell's** collection, assigned by
Lydekker to *C. gouldi* (an obsolete name for the swamp harrier), are described as
belonging to a **more robust bird**. That material may now be lost, but it may well be
the first Eyles' harrier remains ever collected.

> ### The bit that matters for this project
>
> **Forbes' type material is North Island (Te Aute). Scarlett's is South Island
> (Pyramid Valley).** And NZ Birds Online notes that **North Island birds were markedly
> larger**, with two species suspected.
>
> So **if the two forms are ever formally split, *C. teauteensis* stays with the North
> Island bird — the larger one, ours — and *eylesi* would be resurrected for the South
> Island form.** The Manawatū bird carries the senior name *and* is the big one.

## 3. Where it came from — the ice ages built it

Knapp et al. (2019) sequenced mitogenomes from both New Zealand giant raptors and
overturned the earlier assumption.

- **Closest living relative: the spotted harrier (*Circus assimilis*) of Australia** —
  a much smaller bird. **Not** the swamp harrier, which earlier hypotheses had favoured.
- The two diverged around **2.4 million years ago**.
- **Both** Eyles' harrier and Haast's eagle descend from **small, open-land-adapted
  Australasian relatives** that arrived in the **late Pliocene to early Pleistocene**
  — Haast's eagle from the little eagle (*Hieraaetus morphnoides*), similarly tiny.
- Their arrival **coincided with the development of open habitat in New Zealand** as the
  climate shifted and forest gave way to open country. With no mammalian predators to
  compete with, both lineages **increased in size rapidly** — textbook island gigantism.

> `[BUILD]` **The installation's climate cycle is the reason its apex predator exists at
> that size.** The same Pleistocene opening-up that Deep Dive 4 documents — frost, wind,
> a two-thirds-open glacial landscape — is what a small Australian harrier arrived into
> and grew to fill. That is a direct, sourced link between the land narrative and the
> bird flying over it, and it is a much better story than "big extinct hawk."

**On span:** the lineage has been here ~2 Ma, so it comfortably passes the 1 Ma
criterion. Be slightly careful about claiming it was *already giant* at the old end of
the window — the gigantism evolved after arrival — but by 345 ka it is long established.

## 4. What it was

| | |
|---|---|
| **Mass** | **3.0–3.5 kg** overall; adult female **2.5–3 kg**. Over twice a swamp harrier — up to **four times** by some measures |
| **Wingspan** | ~2 m — the size of a **small golden eagle** |
| **Talons** | roughly **50% larger** than a swamp harrier's |
| **Build** | **Compact wings, powerful long legs.** Its shape differed so much from other harriers that **it was initially mistaken for a huge hawk — possibly a giant *Accipiter*** |
| **Senses** | skull structure indicates **highly directional, owl-like hearing** |
| **Geography** | Wairarapa to Coromandel in the NI; Canterbury northwards in the SI. **Sea level to the subalpine zone** |
| **Habitat** | **dry forest and shrubland** |
| **Nesting** | prey remains at **rock shelters and cave entrances** suggest it nested there |
| **Population** | *"unknown but probably sparse"* |

**The single most important line:** it was **more adapted to avian prey**, since apart
from bats there were no mammals to hunt, and it *"presumably hunted diurnal birds in a
manner similar to goshawks."* Compact wings and long legs are ambush-and-dash
equipment, not soaring equipment.

---

## 5. What it ate

From a North Island midden accumulation and general morphology:

- **Kererū — the favoured prey.**
- **North Island kōkako, kākā, and Finsch's duck** also recovered.
- A **generalist** taking land animals of **one to a few kilograms**.
- North Island birds were large enough to take **moa up to 40 kg**.

That 40 kg ceiling is worth holding onto. A *Dinornis* female of the genus runs to
**150–250 kg**. So **adult giant moa were never on the menu** — the harrier's moa
predation is on **chicks, juveniles, and the smaller species** (*Anomalopteryx* at
26–64 kg sits right on the boundary; *Pachyornis geranoides*, the smallest NI moa, sits
well inside it).

---

## 6. Ecological roles

Six, and several of them are useful to the sim.

1. **Sole apex predator of the North Island.** Haast's eagle was South-Island-only, so
   there is no second large raptor here. Top-down pressure across the whole bird fauna
   sat on this one species.
2. **A predator of *flighted* birds — which is unusual for an island apex.** Most
   island top predators specialise on flightless prey. Kērangi's favourite food was a
   pigeon. So its pressure fell on the **canopy**, not just the forest floor.
3. **A constraint on moa *recruitment*, not moa *survival*.** With a 40 kg ceiling it
   removed eggs, chicks and juveniles. That is a limit on population growth rate rather
   than on adult mortality — which is precisely the shape an age-structured predator–prey
   model needs, and it's already what the engine's egg and juvenile stages support.
4. **An indirect hand on forest regeneration.** Its favourite prey, kererū, is the
   **only** disperser of large fruit (Dive 5 §3.3). Predator → pigeon → podocarp and
   tawa recruitment is a real three-link chain, all of it sourced.
5. **A bone concentrator.** Prey remains accumulate at its roosts and nest sites — like
   the laughing owl, it is part of *why there is a fossil record at all*.
6. **A product of the glacial cycle.** §3. It is the ice ages made flesh.

---

## 7. Why it lost — and the irony

It went extinct **within a few centuries of Polynesian arrival**, from a combination of:

- **Habitat conversion.** Its dry forest and shrubland was burned off and replaced with
  grassland.
- **Prey collapse.** Pacific rat predation reduced its prey base.
- **Direct hunting.** Its bones appear in middens, and were **worked into tools**.
- **Chick predation** by Pacific rat and Polynesian dog.

**The irony is exact.** Its ancestors were **open-country harriers**. It became a
forest-and-shrubland ambush hunter, trading soaring wings for compact ones. When humans
turned New Zealand back into open grassland, **it was too heavy and small-winged for the
gliding and soaring that open-country hunting requires.** The habitat its lineage came
from returned, and it could no longer use it.

### And this is why the swamp harrier can't appear

Not merely a date. **A mechanism.**

Swamp harrier *female* bones are hard to tell from Eyles' harrier *male* bones — they
differ little except in stoutness. But **every swamp harrier remain studied in detail
from New Zealand is at most about 1,000 years old.** There is **no evidence of
widespread coexistence**. Despite their size difference the two were ecologically
similar enough to **competitively exclude one another**, and *C. approximans* could only
establish once kērangi was gone.

`[BUILD]` So the swamp harrier isn't just anachronistic in our window — **its presence
would be mutually exclusive with the bird we're building.**

## 8. Design notes, with their evidence

| # | Design note | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Hunt like a goshawk, not a harrier.** Fast dashes from cover or a perch; use forest and forest-edge structure. **No sustained soaring or wide circling.** | Compact wings, long powerful legs; shape so unlike a harrier it was mistaken for a giant *Accipiter*; "hunted diurnal birds in a manner similar to goshawks"; "fast flyer capable of hunting flighted birds in forest habitats" |
| **2** | **Prey is birds; moa are a subset with a hard 40 kg ceiling.** Target eggs, chicks, juveniles and small species. **Never an adult giant.** | Kererū favoured; kōkako, kākā, Finsch's duck in a NI midden; generalist at 1–few kg; NI birds able to take moa up to 40 kg |
| **3** | **Don't shrink it in the cold.** Its habitat *is* the glacial mosaic — hold or raise its numbers as the landscape opens, and shift its prey mix from kererū toward Finsch's duck and small moa. | "Widespread in dry forest and shrubland… sea level to the subalpine zone"; descended from an open-land-adapted ancestor; **evolved giant as New Zealand opened up** |
| **4** | **Keep it sparse.** Few individuals, large ranges. Tune the predator:prey ratio to a small absolute number. | "Population unknown but probably sparse"; apex, large-bodied, presumably territorial |
| **5** | **Anchor the nest to terrain, not to a tree** — bluffs, gorge walls, the greywacke edge. | Prey remains at **rock shelters and cave entrances** suggest it nested at such sites |
| **6** | **Storms should ground it.** The existing "storm distracts the predator" behaviour is **better justified for this species than it would be for an eagle** — a heavy, compact-winged forest hunter is precisely the bird a gale takes out of the air. | Compact wings, 3–3.5 kg, forest hunter; and it could not manage the sustained flight open-country hunting demands |
| **7** | **Never render a swamp harrier.** | Competitive exclusion, §7 |
| **8** | **Consider giving it a hearing cue.** If the sim ever conveys detection radius, an owl-like one is defensible and distinctive. | Skull structure indicates highly directional, owl-like hearing |

### One optional flourish

Harriers are **reverse-sexually-dimorphic** — females larger than males — and the
figures here reflect that (female 2.5–3 kg within a 3.0–3.5 kg range). That's the same
trick as *Dinornis* and huia, though the difference is far less dramatic and probably
not worth a second sprite unless the art budget is generous.

## 9. To verify

1. **Is kērangi the "extinct predator" in the Te Ahu a Tūranga assemblage?** Still the
   highest-value open question in the project — it would let the installation say
   *"this bird, from this ground."* A call to Te Manawa and Dr Holdaway, not a
   literature search.
2. **Which name to display.** *C. teauteensis* is current (OSNZ 2022) and is the North
   Island name. But **kērangi** is the te reo name NZ Birds Online carries, and for a
   museum screen that is likely the one to lead with — subject to mana whenua co-design,
   like everything else in the naming layer.
3. **Whether the NI/SI split has advanced** since the 2022 checklist. If it has, our
   bird may acquire a subspecies epithet.
4. **Timing of the gigantism.** The lineage arrived ~2 Ma; when it reached full size is
   less well constrained. Fine at 345 ka, but don't over-claim if the window is ever
   pushed further back.
